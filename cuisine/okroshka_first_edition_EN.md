## Abstract

This study analyzes okroshka as a complex culinary system in which dish identity is determined not by any single ingredient but by a combination of physical and chemical parameters: the type of acidic liquid base, serving temperature, the dicing calibre of the solid fraction, and the composition of aromatic herbs. In contrast to the dictionary definition (“a cold soup on kvass made from chopped meat, onion, and other seasonings”), the study shows that kvass is only one carrier of the acidic-base function, alongside fermented-milk products and acidified water, while the constitutive features of the class are structural rather than ingredient-based: cold serving and a finely diced mixture. The chemistry of the liquid bases (kvass, kefir and related products, acidified water, whey) is examined, including organic acids, carbonation, and buffer capacity; serving temperature is analyzed as an independent sensory axis on the basis of data on the temperature dependence of taste; and dicing is treated as a factor in the release of fresh-cut aroma compounds. A first parametric classification of okroshka variants is proposed along six functional axes (base, coldness, dicing, solid filling, acidity, herbs), with a formal type code and a similarity metric between variants. A theoretical model of an “okroshka-ness index” (ИО) is formulated with a necessary filter on the physical axes (Х ≥ 0.5 and Н ≥ 0.5) and a core threshold, while explicitly distinguishing two levels: structural okroshka-ness and historical-cultural affiliation with the family. Eight falsifiable hypotheses (H1–H8) are proposed, including multimodality of the category with a hypothesis of two principal prototypical centers (kvass-based and kefir-based), the sensory significance of carbonation, and a compensatory architecture of the axes. A program of experimental testing is developed using objective metrics (pH measurement, titratable acidity, HPLC of organic acids, and sensory analysis with a latent variable of category membership). The study is theoretical and model-based; empirical validation is left for future research.

**Keywords:** okroshka; kvass; kefir; cold soups; acidity; carbonation; trigeminal system; temperature and taste; culinary classification; sensory analysis; culinary history.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1. Statement of the problem

In the dictionary tradition, okroshka is defined through kvass and chopping: “a cold soup on kvass, made from chopped meat, onion, and other seasonings” [1]. The definition identifies three features—coldness, kvass, and fine chopping—yet culinary practice systematically exceeds these boundaries. There are okroshka variants:

- based on fermented-milk products (kefir, ayran, cultured sour milk, buttermilk), without kvass;
- based on mineral water with an added acidifier (lemon juice, vinegar), without a fermented base;
- based on whey, brine, and their mixtures with kvass—historically documented [3], [6];
- with fish instead of meat (historical fish okroshka) [6], [7];
- fasting variants without a protein filling, and egg-and-vegetable variants;
- with beet and beet tops, at the boundary with cold beet soup and botvinya.

At the same time, bearers of the culinary tradition unhesitatingly assign most of these dishes to a single class—and just as readily deny that status to formally similar dishes: a cold kefir-and-beet soup (šaltibarščiai [11]) is called “cold borscht,” not okroshka, although structurally it nearly coincides with kefir okroshka. This yields the central research question:

> **Which features make a dish okroshka?**

Methodologically, the study completes a triad of paired investigations. The shchi study [30] described a category without a binary threshold, in which acidity is an optional axis; the borscht study [31] described a category with a necessary flavor filter (K ≥ 0.5 and S₁ ≥ 0.5). Okroshka provides a third configuration: a class with a necessary filter on **physical** axes—serving temperature and mixture structure—while its flavor axis (the acidity of the base) is variable and permits substitution of its carrier. A comparison of the three configurations is reserved for a joint methodological paper within the project; here it serves to define the problem.

### 1.2. Etymology and historical context

**Etymology.** The name of the dish refers not to an ingredient but to an operation: *okroshka* derives from *kroshit’*—to crumble, chop, or cut finely. In Dahl’s dictionary, the entry appears in the word family around “okrašivat’ (obkraŝivat’), o(b)krošit’—to break off around,” with the Tver example “we have already *okroshilis’*—finished chopping the cabbage,” while the dish itself is defined as “a cold soup on kvass, made from chopped meat, onion, and other seasonings” [1]. Thus the lexicon itself already fixes two constitutive features of the class that the present study treats as axes: cold serving and fine chopping. The third feature in the definition—kvass—will prove historically replaceable.

**Genealogy: tyurya and botvinya.** Okroshka developed within a family of cold kvass-based dishes that required no fire. The simplest of these was tyurya: bread or rusks crumbled into water, kvass, cultured sour milk, or milk, with salt and a small quantity of vegetable oil; until the nineteenth century it was an everyday peasant dish [9]. The older “aristocratic” form was botvinya: a cold kvass soup made from cooked leafy tops, onion, cucumber, and fish (Dahl’s definition), with a complex acidic architecture—kvass, sorrel, a rye scald starter, grated horseradish, and lemon juice “for pungency and aroma” [8]. Okroshka occupies an intermediate position within this family: a dense diced mixture (as in tyurya, but not bread-based) in an acidic base (as in botvinya, but without cooked greens).

**The first printed recipes.** The earliest documented culinary description of okroshka dates to 1790 and belongs to Nikolai Osipov (*Starinnaya russkaya khozyaika, klyuchnitsa i stryapukha*): “chop various roasted meats with onion, cucumbers, and sour cream, and, pouring over cucumber brine, or kvass, or shchi, serve chilled” [2]. The recipe is noteworthy in three respects at once: sour cream is already present in the earliest record of the dish (a fermented-milk component is therefore not a Soviet innovation); the base is given as an explicit series of permissible substitutions—“brine / kvass / sour shchi”; and the instruction to serve it chilled establishes the coldness axis as obligatory already in the first recipe. Several years later, in Vasily Levshin’s *Slovar’ povarennyi*, the dish is prepared “from leftovers of various roasted meats—quadrupeds, domestic and wild birds,” with a mixture of fresh and salted cucumbers, salted plums, and onion; the liquid portion is not pure kvass but a mixture of plum and cucumber brines, apple vinegar, and kvass, while potatoes are absent or served separately, fried [3], [4]. In Molokhovets (1861), okroshka appears as an established recipe with chopped meat, cucumbers, herbs, and kvass [5]. The Brockhaus and Efron Encyclopedic Dictionary records a remarkable detail: the okroshka mixture is to be “diluted with kvass **or sour shchi**,” while fried fish is recommended for fasting okroshka (without sour cream and eggs) [6]—an early documented substitution of the base that directly connects the present study to the paired study on shchi [30]. (Here and below, “sour shchi” in quotations means an acidic sauerkraut-based liquid—a soup—not the historical malt-and-honey beverage of the same name; the polysemy of the term is discussed in the paired study [30, §5.4].) Pokhlebkin states that a separate **white (okroshka) kvass** was traditionally prepared for okroshka from raw rye malt and flour: markedly more acidic and fuller-bodied than drinking bread kvass, with no added sugar, sometimes flavored with mint or horseradish [7].

**Soviet canonization and the kefir branch.** Two shifts occurred in the twentieth century. First, standardized public catering established okroshka as a mass summer dish with Doktorskaya cooked sausage, potatoes, and radish (recipes were already collected in the 1939 *Book of Tasty and Healthy Food* [12]). Second, the spread of industrial fermented-milk products generated a second major branch of the class—kefir okroshka—which by the end of the twentieth century had become a fully developed second center of the category rather than a marginal variant. The post-Soviet period added a third, “compromise” base: mineral water with lemon juice or vinegar. The “kvass / kefir / mineral water” fork is the principal internal dispute of the modern category, and the present study shifts that dispute from the domain of opinion to that of measurement.

**Folk historiography and its verification.** A stable layer of folk accounts has grown up around the early history of okroshka and circulates in culinary publications without references to sources. Three of the most common claims can be directly checked. (1) “Okroshka has been known since the fourteenth century because kvass appeared then.” Chronology disproves this claim: kvass is documented in Russian written sources no later than the late tenth century (the first mention is in the *Primary Chronicle*, under 989/996: Vladimir’s baptism was accompanied by the distribution to the people of “food, mead, and kvass” [26]). Thus a date “from the fourteenth century” understates the age of the carrier by four centuries, but by itself does not prove the existence of the dish. (2) “Proto-okroshka existed before kvass, on cucumber or cabbage brine.” This version is chemically plausible (lactic-acid brine is a functional equivalent of kvass on axis K; see §4.1), but it is not documented: the earliest written evidence for the dish remains Osipov’s 1790 recipe [2], while the silence of fourteenth- to eighteenth-century sources (*Domostroi*, household expenditure books) is a fact, not proof of the dish’s absence (see the evidence table). (3) “Okroshka was invented by Volga burlaks, who crumbled dried fish and radish into kvass.” This narrative has no documentary support and is marked [Г] as a legend; nevertheless, it accurately describes the target function of the dish (§2.1): cold assembly from storage-stable components without access to fire. The status of all three accounts within this study is [Г]: they must not be placed on the evidential ladder, but they may be used to motivate experimental design (for example, testing a brine-based variant in the pilot subseries of Experiment 2).

**Caveat on evidential value.** The historical evidence in this section is directly relevant to the model, but its status must be stated precisely. Etymology and documentary history provide a **historical argument that the core of the class lies in structure and base rather than in any particular filling**, not proof of the structure of the modern dish. The fact that Levshin diluted the mixture with brines and kvass does not imply that modern okroshka is defined by brine. The latter question must be tested by chemistry and experiment (§§3, 7), not by etymology. The same principle is adopted in the paired studies [30], [31]: the historical-cultural review functions as corroborating context rather than as the basis of the conclusions.

### 1.4. Aim, objectives, and status of the study

**Aim.** To describe okroshka as a multifactor culinary system and construct a model that measures the **degree of structural similarity between a dish and the prototypical core of the okroshka family**, rather than searching for a minimal set of necessary ingredients—which does not exist for a historically evolved family.

**Objectives:**

1. Identify and describe the functional parameters of the system (base, coldness, dicing, filling, acidity, herbs).
2. Construct a classification of okroshka variants by these parameters, including historical and borderline forms.
3. Analyze the liquid base as an independent variable using data on chemical composition.
4. Examine functional substitutes for the base, including historical precedents.
5. Formulate a theoretical model (the “okroshka-ness index”) with a necessary filter and threshold.
6. Develop a program of experimental testing with objective metrics.

**Status of the study.** This is a theoretical and model-based investigation; experimental data specifically on okroshka are absent from the literature, and no original experiment has yet been conducted. Three levels of claims are distinguished and marked throughout: **[Д]**—established by sources; **[П]**—transfer of evidence from adjacent domains to okroshka (plausible but untested); **[Г]**—the author’s own hypothesis. The labeling and presentation are harmonized with the paired studies [30], [31].

## 2. Materials and Methods

The study was conducted by theoretical analysis and modeling and consists of four stages:

1. **Analysis of the scientific literature**—on the chemistry and microbiology of fermented beverages (kvass, kefir), the temperature dependence of taste perception, perception of carbonation and trigeminal mechanisms of pungency, formation of aroma compounds when vegetables are cut, and historical culinary sources (dictionaries, cookbooks, regulatory documents).
2. **Comparative classification**—systematization of known okroshka variants by the selected parameters, including historical forms (Levshin, Molokhovets, sour shchi as a base) and borderline forms (botvinya, tyurya, cold beet soup, šaltibarščiai, tarator).
3. **Modeling**—construction of a formalized “okroshka-ness index” (ИО) with a necessary filter, threshold, and explicit status as an a priori model.
4. **Experimental-series design**—development of a reproducible methodology with objective metrics: pH measurement and titratable acidity, buffer capacity (following the protocol of the paired study [30]), HPLC of organic acids, CO₂ content, colorimetry, and a sensory panel with three indicators of the latent category-membership variable (binary identification, graded similarity 0–10, confidence 0–3), with training and validation subsets split by batch and day.

**Source-critical note.** Scientific sources were selected through Google Scholar (September 2026) and labeled by evidential strength ([Д]/[П]/[Г], see §1.4). The historical-culinary block [1]–[12] includes primary sources (dictionaries, cookbooks, regulatory documents) and works by food historians; these are identified as such and separated in the text from experimental data, as in the paired studies [30], [31]. Regulatory standards (GOST) are used as sources for physicochemical specifications of industrial bases—kvass [13] and kefir [14]—not as culinary prescriptions.

## 3. Principal Parameters of Okroshka as a System

Okroshka is treated as a system of six functional axes and two modifiers. The axes enter the index model (§8); the modifiers (carbonation and pungent seasoning) tune perception but do not determine class membership.

### 3.1. Liquid base (axis O)

**[Д]** The liquid base carries acidity, sweetness, dissolved/extractive solids, and—when fermented—carbonation. Historically, it is the axis along which the principal bifurcation of the class has occurred. Three major types can be distinguished:

1. **Kvass.** A product of co-fermentation by lactic acid bacteria and yeasts: commercial kvass is dominated by *Lactobacillus casei*, *Leuconostoc mesenteroides*, and *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, while its metabolite profile includes lactate, acetate, and ethanol [15]. Under GOST 31494-2012, kvass is standardized for acidity (1.5–7.0 acidity units), soluble solids (at least 3.5%), alcohol (no more than 1.2%), and carbon dioxide (at least 0.30% for draft kvass) [13]. The historical specialized form is white (okroshka) kvass: more acidic and less sweet, without added sugar [7]. Kvass is the only base that simultaneously supplies acidity, extractive body, rye aroma, and carbonation.
2. **Fermented-milk bases** (kefir, ayran, cultured sour milk, buttermilk). Kefir is produced by symbiotic fermentation of milk by lactic acid bacteria and yeasts organized in kefir grains; its standardized acidity is 85–130 °T, gas formation by the microbiota is permitted [14], and its microbiota and metabolites (lactic acid, acetaldehyde, diacetyl, traces of ethanol and CO₂) are described in review literature [16]. Unlike fat-free kvass, a fermented-milk base adds a fat phase and protein to the system.
3. **Acidified water and other bases** (mineral water with lemon juice or vinegar, whey, brine, and mixtures of brine with kvass—the historical Levshin variant [3]). Here acidity is created by an added substance rather than fermentation: the profile lacks the accompanying metabolites (sugars, amino acids, volatile compounds), which separates this group from fermented bases—a distinction that can be measured instrumentally (§7, Experiment 2).

**[П]** Sensory transfer: by analogy with the borscht model [31], where the acid source determines the acid profile but is not identical to perceived sourness, the type of base (a coordinate in the code, §4.4) and the intensity of acidity (axis K, §3.5) are deliberately separated.

### 3.2. Coldness (axis Х)

**[Д]** Okroshka is a cold soup: serving temperature is 4–12 °C, extending to the addition of ice [6], [10]. Coldness is not an external circumstance of serving but a sensory-active parameter. The temperature dependence of taste has been studied experimentally: perceived intensity of tastants depends on the temperature of both sample and tongue [19], while the molecular mechanism has been partly elucidated—thermal activation of the TRPM5 channel in the 15–35 °C range enhances responses to sweet, umami, and bitter stimuli [18]. **[П]** Applied to okroshka, cooling suppresses the perceived sweetness of the base and softens bitter-pungent notes, while acidity at low temperature is perceived as “refreshing” rather than “heavy.” Thus coldness is not merely a background condition but an **embedded regulator of the system’s flavor balance**: the same base at 8 °C and at 40 °C constitutes a different sensory object.

**[Г]** For this reason, temperature is advanced as a constitutive axis of the class and included in the model’s necessary filter (§8): a dish served hot loses its identity as okroshka regardless of composition—a prediction testable by a mirror condition (H2, §7). In the paired studies, temperature is only a protocol covariate (shchi and borscht are served hot at controlled temperature [30], [31]); in okroshka, for the first time in the project, it becomes a structural axis.

### 3.3. Carbonation (modifier Г)

**[Д]** Fermented bases contain dissolved CO₂ (at least 0.30% by mass for draft kvass [13]; gas formation is normatively permitted in kefir [14]). Carbonation perception has a dual nature: somatosensory (mechanical stimulation by bubbles) and gustatory proper—CO₂ is detected by sour-sensing taste cells through the enzyme carbonic anhydrase 4, meaning that the “taste of carbonation” is physiologically related to sour taste [17]. **[П]** Applied to okroshka, carbonation in kvass and kefir increases perceived “refreshment” and interacts with the acidity axis: a degassed base should yield measurably different typicality ratings at the same titratable acidity (H5). Carbonation is classified as a modifier rather than an axis because it varies within a single base type (fresh vs flat kvass) and does not differentiate variants of the class.

### 3.4. Pungent seasoning (modifier Тр)

**[Д]** Traditional pungent additions to okroshka are table mustard and grated horseradish [6], [7]. Their pungency is produced by isothiocyanates—primarily allyl isothiocyanate in horseradish and mustard—which arise from glucosinolates when plant tissue is damaged and act on the TRPA1 trigeminal channel of sensory neurons [20]. Radish (also Brassicaceae) contains its own glucosinolates and therefore contributes a weaker pungent note of the same kind directly to the diced mixture. **[П]** Applied to okroshka, mustard-horseradish seasoning and radish form the dish’s **trigeminal circuit**, a second non-gustatory sensory channel after carbonation; in this respect okroshka is the most “trigeminal” dish in the project triad (the trigeminal component was not separated out in the shchi and borscht studies [30], [31]). **[Г]** Prediction: with the base held constant, addition of a standardized mustard-horseradish note increases typicality ratings (H5).

### 3.5. Acidity (axis K)

**[Д]** Acidity is always present in okroshka, but its carrier and origin vary: fermentative (kvass—lactate and acetate [15]; kefir—predominantly lactate [16]) or added (lemon juice, vinegar, brine [3]). As in the paired studies, two acidity scales are distinguished: **chemical** (pH and titratable acidity, directly measurable; kvass is standardized at 1.5–7.0 acidity units [13], kefir at 85–130 °T [14]) and **sensory** (perceived sourness, dependent on buffer capacity, temperature, sweetness, and the fat phase). The method for measuring buffer capacity (titration with standard alkali to a fixed pH endpoint) is transferred unchanged from the paired study [30], which also defines the buffer-sensitivity coefficient βₚ = Δ(added acid)/ΔpH, allowing bases to be compared in their resistance to acidification. **[П]** Okroshka acidity is governed by the same four mechanisms for controlling perceived sourness systematized in the shchi study [30]: dilution (with water or mineral water), masking (with sour cream or kvass sugar), neutralization, and compensation (with mustard-horseradish and salty notes).

### 3.6. Dicing (axis Н)

**[Д]** Dicing is the only axis encoded in the dish’s very name (“to chop/crumble” [1]): the solid fraction (cucumbers, boiled potatoes, protein filling, egg, radish) is cut into small cubes or strips on the order of 3–7 mm, while the herbs are chopped. Dicing has a direct chemical consequence: tissue damage initiates enzymatic formation of “fresh-cut” aroma compounds. In cucumber, the characteristic aroma is determined by nine-carbon aldehydes—especially (E,Z)-2,6-nonadienal—formed through the lipoxygenase pathway from unsaturated fatty acids [22]; in onion, cutting releases volatile sulfur compounds from S-alk(en)yl cysteine sulfoxides [21]. **[П]** Applied to okroshka, the finer and fresher the cut, the more intense the aroma of the mixture and the more evenly it is distributed in the base; coarse cutting shifts perception toward “salad covered with liquid,” i.e. toward the class boundary (H3). Dicing is therefore included in the model’s necessary filter alongside coldness (§8).

### 3.7. Solid filling (axis П)

**[Д]** The protein- and starch-rich solid fraction has historically varied: boiled or roasted mixed meats [3], ham and sausage (the Soviet norm [12]), lightly salted or fried fish [6], egg, and boiled potatoes (a late component—still absent in Levshin or served separately, fried [3], [4]). The filling supplies satiety and an umami background. **Umami** (from Japanese *umai*, “delicious”) is the fifth basic taste, commonly described as “savory” or “brothy”; its carriers are glutamate and certain nucleotides present in meat, fish, and egg [23]. In okroshka, the umami background is weaker than in the hot soups of the triad because there is no broth extraction, and axis П therefore receives a moderate weight in the model (§8). The treatment of umami is harmonized across the triad: it is incorporated within existing axes (here, axis П; in ИЩ and ИБ, axes N and O respectively) and is not separated into an independent axis in any of the three models; whether it should be separated is a shared open question of the research program [30, §9].

### 3.8. Herbs and aroma (axis З)

**[Д]** Fresh herbs constitute an obligatory aromatic layer: scallion, dill, fresh cucumber, radish; historical recipes add tarragon and aromatic herbs [6], while white kvass was infused with mint and horseradish [7]. The chemistry of this layer consists of fresh-cut volatile compounds (§3.6) and the mild pungency of radish (§3.4). **[П]** Axis З is the principal carrier of the dish’s “freshness” and therefore interacts with axis Х: coldness and green aromatics reinforce one another in the perception of “refreshment.”

### 3.9. Accompaniments (layer M—external modifier)

As in the paired studies [30], [31], accompaniments are treated as an **external cultural modifier** rather than a structural axis: sour cream (or mayonnaise, a later substitute) added to the bowl; mustard and horseradish served separately; ice; and hot boiled potatoes alongside the cold soup—a ritual brought to canonical form in the Lithuanian tradition of šaltibarščiai [11]. Sour cream, as in shchi, is dual in function: a serving element and a final regulator of perceived acidity and thickness (masking in the taxonomy of [30]). The functional logic of accompaniments is shared across the triad: fatty and starchy additions soften the acidic axis, while pungent additions let the diner raise the trigeminal tone individually.

## 4. Classification of Okroshka Variants

### 4.1. Existing approaches

**[Д]** Available systematizations of okroshka are of two types. **Dictionary definitions** fix a prototype (“kvass + chopped meat + herbs, served cold” [1]) but do not describe internal diversity. **Enumerative lists** in cookbooks and recipe collections list variants by name (“meat okroshka,” “kefir okroshka,” “fasting okroshka,” “fish okroshka” [5], [12]) without identifying the system of features that generates this diversity. Both approaches encounter the same dead end identified for borscht in the paired study [31]: listing variants does not explain why bearers of the tradition assign them to one class or where the class boundary lies.

### 4.3. Main classification

**[Г]** On the axes defined in §3, the following types are distinguished:

1. **Classic meat okroshka on kvass**—the class prototype: white or bread kvass, meat filling, cucumber, herbs, sour cream [1], [5], [7].
2. **Fasting / fish okroshka on kvass (historical)**—kvass, fish (lightly salted or fried), or a vegetable set without meat and eggs [6], [7].
3. **Kefir (fermented-milk) okroshka**—the Soviet/post-Soviet second center of the class; kefir, ayran, cultured sour milk [12] and contemporary practice.
4. **Okroshka on acidified water** (“on mineral water”)—a late compromise form with added acidity.
5. **Okroshka on whey / brine**—marginal contemporary forms with historical precedent (Levshin’s mixtures of brines and kvass [3]).
6. **Mixed-base okroshka**—kvass + sour cream in the base (rather than as a serving addition), kefir + mineral water (dilution): intermediate positions between types 1 and 3.
7. **Beet okroshka (okroshka with beet)**—a borderline form: the diced structure of okroshka with beet or beet tops added; in the paired study it was separated from borscht on axis T as an “assembled (okroshka-type) cold soup” [31]; in the present model it is a member of the class approaching the boundary with cold beet soup (§8.1).
8. **Genealogical forms outside the class:** tyurya (bread crumbs as a different carrier of the solid fraction) and botvinya (cooked greens as a different processing mode) [8], [9]; **convergent forms:** tarator [10], šaltibarščiai [11], gazpacho, as well as Azerbaijani ovdukh and dovga [27; 28] and Belarusian kholodnik [29]—structural neighbors from other traditions (§8.1).

### 4.4. Type code

**[Г]** For formal notation of variants, a five-coordinate type code is proposed:

**Ок = (О, Х, Н, П, К)**

where:

- **О (liquid base):** О1—bread kvass; О2—white (okroshka) kvass; О3—kefir; О4—cultured sour milk / ayran / buttermilk; О5—acidified water; О6—whey / brine; О7—mixed;
- **Х (serving temperature):** Х1—4–12 °C (cold, with or without ice); Х2—room temperature (permitted briefly); Х3—warm/hot (outside the class);
- **Н (dicing):** Н1—fine (3–7 mm); Н2—medium (8–12 mm); Н3—coarse (>12 mm); Н4—grated / puréed;
- **П (solid filling):** П1—boiled/roasted meat; П2—sausage/ham; П3—fish; П4—egg-and-vegetable (fasting); П5—mixed;
- **К (source of acidity):** К1—fermentative, kvass-derived; К2—fermented-milk; К3—added (citric acid, lemon juice); К4—vinegar; К5—brine; К6—mixed.

The code is written, for example, as “Ок(О1; Х1; Н1; П1; К1)” for classic meat okroshka on kvass and “Ок(О3; Х1; Н1; П2; К2)” for kefir okroshka with sausage. Coordinate К in the code records the **origin** of acidity, whereas axis K in the index records its **intensity** (§8): this separation mirrors the paired borscht study (acid source in code Б, intensity on axis K [31]). Serving ritual and accompaniments are not included in the code; they form an external cultural modifier (layer M, §3.9).

### 4.5. Similarity metric between variants

**[Г]** By analogy with the paired study [31], a formal measure of difference between variants is introduced. **The baseline is Hamming distance across code coordinates:** two variants are closer the more coordinates they share; distance d_H ranges from 0 (complete match) to 5 (difference on every axis). Rules for composite values (О7, К6) and missing values are transferred from the paired study: a composite coordinate is treated as matching any simple value included in it, and a missing value is excluded from the count.

Hamming distance is intentionally coarse: it does not distinguish “near” from “far” values within a coordinate—for example, replacing kvass with kefir and replacing kvass with vinegar-acidified water incur the same penalty. Therefore a **typed generalization** is defined as d_f = Σⱼ wⱼ·dⱼ(xⱼ, yⱼ), where dⱼ is the ordinal distance within a coordinate (for Н, the difference in dicing calibre; for Х, the difference between temperature classes), and wⱼ are the model’s axis weights. **Criterion for accepting the added complexity:** the typed metric replaces the baseline only if it predicts the panel responses in Experiment 3 (variant similarity) statistically better; otherwise Hamming distance is retained—the same principle of parsimony used in the paired study [31].

## 5. The Liquid Base as an Independent Variable

**[Д → П]** Mirroring the section “Beet Types as an Independent Variable” in the paired study [31], the liquid base is treated as a variable manipulated while the diced mixture is held constant. Available carriers differ in measurable chemical parameters:

| Base | Type of acidity | Dominant acids | CO₂ | Solids / fat | Regulatory framework |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bread kvass | fermentative | lactic, acetic [15] | ≥ 0.30% [13] | ≥ 3.5% solids, no fat [13] | GOST 31494-2012 |
| White (okroshka) kvass | fermentative | lactic, acetic; no added sugar [7] | present | more extractive than drinking kvass [7] | historical technology [7] |
| Kefir | fermented-milk | lactic [16] | permitted [14] | fat 0.1–9.5%, protein ≥ 2.6% [14] | GOST 31454-2012 |
| Cultured sour milk / ayran / buttermilk | fermented-milk | lactic | none–weak | variable | — |
| Acidified water | added | citric / acetic | if carbonated | ≈ 0 | — |
| Whey (dairy) | fermentative | lactic | none | low | — |
| Brine (cucumber, cabbage) | fermentative + salt | lactic, acetic | none | low, high mineralization | historical precedent [3] |

**[П]** Each row in the table constitutes an experimental condition in Experiments 1 and 2 (§7). The model predicts that: (1) replacing the base shifts the flavor profile along axes O and K and modifier Г, but does not destroy dish identity as long as the filter (Х, Н) and the acidic function are preserved (H7); (2) fermented bases can be distinguished from directly acidified ones by their accompanying metabolites (sugars, amino acids, volatile compounds) even when pH and titratable acidity are matched—the contrast between levels Б/В/Г of Experiment 2; and (3) a kefir base changes not only acidity but also the fat phase, which partly explains the division of the tradition into two camps (H4).

**Comparing bases with the toolkit of the paired study.** Acid profiles of the bases are compared using vector A = (I, C, D, P, R) from [30]: I—intensity; C—acid composition (HPLC data); D—duration of sour sensation, including aftertaste; P—temporal position of acidity (attack, middle, finish); R—character (soft, sharp, astringent, lactic). The typed metric d_A with equal a priori weights of 0.2 is transferred unchanged. **[Г]** Expected result: d_A(kvass, kefir) is significantly greater than zero already on coordinate C (lactate + acetate versus almost pure lactate), whereas d_A(kefir, ayran) is minimal—bases within the fermented-milk group are functionally close.

## 6. Functional Substitutes for the Base

**[Д → П]** Mirroring the section on beet substitutes [31], if kvass is not the sole carrier of the acidic function, then the class permits a ladder of substitutions, each step of which has a historical or practical precedent:

1. **Kvass → kefir / cultured sour milk / ayran**—the principal modern transition (Soviet/post-Soviet branch [12]); it preserves the fermentative origin of acidity, changes the chemical profile, and adds a fat phase.
2. **Kvass → acidified water**—replacement of the carrier while preserving the acidity function, but with loss of metabolite accompaniment and extractive body; the sensory distance from the prototype is maximal among “legal” substitutions (tested in Experiment 3).
3. **Kvass → whey**—a marginal modern substitution, chemically intermediate between the fermented-milk and water-based groups.
4. **Kvass → sour shchi**—a historically documented substitution from the Brockhaus and Efron encyclopedia [6]: an acidic sauerkraut-based liquid used as the base for the okroshka mixture. For the project, this is a direct bridge to the paired study [30] and an argument that the boundary between “soup” and “base” is functional rather than ingredient-based.
5. **Pure kvass → composite base** (brines + vinegar + kvass)—Levshin’s historical norm [3]: the base was originally a tunable mixture rather than a single product.

**[Г]** The substitution ladder generates an empirically testable ordering: expected distance from the prototype increases along “white kvass → bread kvass → kefir → cultured sour milk/ayran → whey → acidified water” (Levshin’s composite bases occupy intermediate positions). This ordering follows directly from the model in §8 and is tested by graded similarity in Experiment 3. **Caveat “object A ≠ object B”** (following the paired study [31]): the historical breadth of the class does not preclude the modern category from having narrowed; the substitution ladder describes the system’s potential, not equal typicality of all steps for a contemporary bearer of the tradition.

## 7. Program of Experimental Testing

### Eight key hypotheses

The model generates eight falsifiable predictions (numbered to mirror the paired borscht study [31]; mirror relations with the hypotheses of the paired studies are stated explicitly):

- **H1.** Type of liquid base is the strongest predictor that a dish will be identified as okroshka: in a logistic regression of identification on the model axes, the coefficient for O exceeds the coefficients for all other axes (mirroring H1 in the borscht study, where acidity is proposed as the strongest predictor [31], and contrasting with the shchi study, where acidity is expected to be the weakest axis [30]).
- **H2.** Temperature is a necessary condition of identification: the same standardized okroshka mixture served at 8 °C and at 60 °C receives fundamentally different proportions of “this is okroshka” responses; under hot serving, identification falls below the peripheral range. Mirror test: in the paired studies, temperature is a controlled covariate rather than a constitutive feature [30], [31]; here it is tested as an axis for the first time.
- **H3.** Dicing calibre affects typicality in a graded manner: a series of three calibres (5 mm / 10 mm / 20 mm) at fixed composition produces monotonically decreasing similarity ratings, while coarse cutting shifts panel descriptions toward “salad covered with liquid.”
- **H4.** The category is multimodal, with a hypothesis of two principal centers: the distribution of typicality ratings separates into centers corresponding to the kvass and kefir branches (and to the taster’s habitual base). Test caveat: a significant “base type × taster’s habitual base” interaction in a mixed model is necessary but not sufficient evidence, because such an interaction can also occur in a single broad distribution. The direct test of multimodality is mixture modeling: comparison of one-, two-, and three-component models of the typicality-rating distribution. The two-center hypothesis is accepted if a two-component model outperforms a one-component model by a pre-specified criterion (BIC or cross-validation), while a third center (the mineral-water/sour-cream branch) is not ruled out a priori.
- **H5.** Carbonation and trigeminal seasoning are sensorially significant: degassed kvass lowers typicality and “refreshment” ratings relative to the same carbonated kvass at equal titratable acidity; addition of a standardized mustard-horseradish note raises typicality ratings at a fixed base.
- **H6.** Cross-class discrimination: the panel distinguishes okroshka from its structural neighbors—botvinya, cold beet soup, šaltibarščiai, tarator, gazpacho; borderline members (beet okroshka, tarator) produce the highest proportion of uncertain responses (mirroring H6 in the borscht study and the cross-class asymmetry test in the shchi study [30], [31]).
- **H7.** Functional substitutability of the base: with the diced mixture fixed and acidity matched, replacement of kvass by kefir (and then by successive steps of the ladder in §6) preserves identification above the peripheral threshold, while graded similarity monotonically orders the ladder steps (motivation and test in §6).
- **H8.** Compensatory architecture: weakening one axis is compensated by strengthening another—a less acidic base paired with a stronger mustard-horseradish note or finer dicing preserves identification at the prototype level (mirroring P6 in the shchi study and H8 in the borscht study [30], [31]).

**Effect sizes.** As in the paired studies, the minimum substantively meaningful effect size is specified in advance: for binary identification, a shift in the proportion of positive responses of at least 0.25 between factor levels; for graded similarity (0–10), a mean shift of at least 1.5 points; for d_A, an effect exceeding the baseline measurement-noise level estimated from repeated samples of the same batch.

### Experiment 0. Pilot survey of obligatory features

**[Г]** Mirroring Experiment 0 in the paired borscht study [31], before the laboratory series, bearers of the tradition (at least 30 participants) are surveyed as to which features they consider obligatory for okroshka (kvass as base, cold serving, fine dicing, meat filling, herbs, acidity, carbonation). The questionnaire is harmonized with the paired studies and includes symmetrical blocks for all three dishes in the triad, providing a comparable empirical baseline of human classification and an external calibration slice for the latent variable in Experiment 3. Expected result: coldness and dicing receive a higher proportion of “obligatory” responses than kvass—a direct pre-laboratory test of the central claim of §8.

### Experiment 1. Chemistry of liquid bases

**[Г]** The objective is to quantify the independent variable from §5. For each base (bread kvass ≥3 samples; white kvass prepared by the historical technology [7]; kefirs at three fat levels; cultured sour milk/ayran; two types of acidified water; whey; brine), measure pH and titratable acidity (to pH 8.1 for fermented-milk products; to pH 6.0 under the protocol of the paired study [30]), buffer capacity and coefficient βₚ [30], HPLC of organic acids (lactate, acetate, citrate, malate), CO₂ content (manometrically/titrimetrically), sugars, solids, and density. Outputs: a table of profiles, pairwise d_A distances among bases, and a map “chemical acidity → sensory sourness” based on a calibration panel using diluted solutions.

### Experiment 2. Four-level design: what carries the acidic contribution of the base

**[Г]** Mirroring the paired studies [30], [31], the design has four levels, with controls:

- **Level A**—real bases (kvass, kefir, acidified water) in a standardized diced mixture (one batch of vegetables, 5 mm calibre, weighed components);
- **Level Б**—model solutions of pure acids (lactic, acetic, citric) in water, matched for pH;
- **Level В**—mixtures of pure acids reproducing the HPLC profile of the real bases (Experiment 1);
- **Level Г**—a real base in a standardized matrix with salt and solids matched.

Contrasts: Б→В estimates the contribution of the acid **profile** (not merely pH); В→Г the contribution of **metabolite accompaniment** (sugars, amino acids, volatile compounds, CO₂); Г→A the contribution of the **matrix** (the kefir fat phase, kvass extractive body). Serving temperature is strictly 8 ± 1 °C (rather than 65 ± 2 °C in the paired studies, reflecting the object); blinding and carryover control follow the general protocol below.

### Experiment 3. Identification and the latent membership variable

**[Г]** A panel of 8–12 tasters evaluates a series of dishes: prototypes using the three bases; borderline members (beet okroshka); genealogical forms (botvinya, tyurya); convergent neighbors (tarator, šaltibarščiai, gazpacho—the cross-tradition negative control, by analogy with gazpacho in the paired study [31]); cross-dish negative controls from the triad—shchi and borscht, each served in two forms (hot at 65 ± 2 °C and cooled to 8 ± 1 °C; the cooled version directly tests whether the panel separates hot soups from okroshka specifically on axis Х, H2); and mirror conditions (prototype at 60 °C; coarse dicing; degassed kvass). Two distinct quantities are recorded for every sample: **membership** (“how much is this okroshka?”), measured by two indicators of a latent variable—binary identification (“okroshka / not okroshka”) and graded similarity (0–10)—and **boundary certainty** (“how certain am I of the classification?”, 0–3). Confidence is deliberately excluded from the latent membership variable: an answer of “definitely not okroshka” with confidence 3 shows that confidence measures certainty about the boundary, not the direction of membership. Uncertainty is quantified by the entropy of the binary decision based on the response proportion p: H = −p·log₂p − (1−p)·log₂(1−p). For canonical okroshka p ≈ 1 and H ≈ 0; for an obvious non-okroshka p ≈ 0 and H ≈ 0; at a true boundary p ≈ 0.5 and H is maximal. Testable consequence: peripheral dishes should show not merely intermediate membership values, but increased entropy together with reduced boundary certainty (H6). **Calibration without circularity:** the sample is split into training and validation subsets by batches and days (not by individual ratings); index weights (logistic regression of identification on axes O, Х, Н, П, K, З) and the ИО threshold (ROC) are estimated only on the training portion and evaluated on the held-out portion. The human panel is treated as an **operational criterion** of category membership (the term “gold standard” is deliberately avoided: 8–12 tasters provide a working standard for laboratory calibration, not a definitive standard of the cultural category itself; population-level validation requires a separate large sample of bearers of the tradition—see the two-panel caveat below), while historical-cultural affiliation is documented separately as an independent external criterion (§8.1). **Separation of panels.** The sensory panel (8–12 trained tasters) is responsible for acidity, aroma, and texture; categorical answers (“is this okroshka?”) are also collected from an expanded panel of bearers of the tradition (at least 30 people, Experiment 0 questionnaire), so cultural experience ceases to be merely a confounder and becomes one of the principal independent variables. **Adversarial subseries:** deliberately constructed counterexamples are included—a sample with formally high ИО that bearers are expected not to call okroshka (for example, a sweet cold dessert mixture on an acidic base), and a canonically named okroshka with deliberately low ИО (a deficient assembly). Disagreement between index and panel on such samples diagnoses holes in the model more strongly than agreement on prototypes. **Generalization test:** in addition to splitting by batches and days, leave-one-recipe-out validation is used—the weights are trained while completely excluding one type of okroshka, after which the model must correctly classify the previously unseen recipe.

### Experiment 4. Culinary morphing: psychometric curve of loss of okroshka-ness

**[Г]** The goal is to measure, rather than stipulate, the class boundaries on the physical axes. The same standardized sample (kvass prototype, 5 mm calibre) is served to the panel at 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 35, 45, and 60 °C (temperature ladder in randomized order); a separate subseries uses a dicing-calibre ladder of 5 → 8 → 12 → 20 mm at a fixed temperature of 8 °C. At each point, binary identification and graded similarity are recorded (Experiment 3 protocol). The result is a **psychometric curve of loss of okroshka-ness**: the proportion of “okroshka” responses as a function of temperature (and of calibre). The curve answers the question of the temperature at which okroshka ceases to be perceived as okroshka and replaces the a priori filter threshold Х ≥ 0.5 with an empirically calibrated one (§8.1); the shape of the curve (step-like or gradual decline) is itself an outcome, testing whether the category has a hard physical boundary. The temperature ladder extends the mirror conditions of Experiment 3 (prototype at 60 °C) and directly parallels the morphing series of the paired studies (acid removal from shchi and borscht [30], [31]) as a third form of controlled transformation of a dish into a non-dish.

### General protocol and statistical plan

The baseline requirement shared across the triad is 5 independent batches of each variant; the minimum acceptable size of a pilot subseries is 3 batches (a batch = a separate preparation of base and mixture on a separate day); sensory panel 8–12 tasters; samples coded with random three-digit codes; serving order randomized with counterbalancing; serving temperature 8 ± 1 °C (thermometer control at the moment of serving); fixed sample volume; water and neutral bread between samples with an interval of at least 2 minutes; some samples assessed repeatedly to test panel reproducibility; and, when the number of variants is large, balanced incomplete blocks to mitigate fatigue. The statistical plan is specified in advance and harmonized with the paired studies [30], [31]: pH and titratable acidity—one-way analysis of variance or the Kruskal–Wallis test; sensory ratings—a mixed-effects model of the form “rating ~ variant + serving order + temperature + (1 | taster) + (1 | batch) + (1 | day)”; identification—logistic regression on the axis features. The number of batches and panel size are justified by power analysis for the effect sizes specified above; if the analysis requires a larger n, the baseline requirement is raised, but never lowered below the common triad level. Multiple comparisons use the Holm correction or FDR control. Bases are correlated through multifunctional carriers (kvass simultaneously covers O, K, and Г; §4.2), so regression includes multicollinearity diagnostics (VIF) and, where necessary, regularization (ridge) or a transition to latent variables. Batches prepared on different days reduce but do not eliminate dependence among replicates (shared raw-material lot, shared kitchen regime), hence the random effect (1 | day), as in the paired studies. Upon completion of the series, open data are published: raw sensory ratings, chemical measurements, and recipe specifications. The experimental series is organized as a three-week restaurant cycle—“shchi week / borscht week / okroshka week”—an extension of the two-week cycle of the paired studies [30], [31]; for okroshka week, serving temperature is lowered in mirror fashion.

The program is divided into a pilot (one prototype base, 3 batches, protocol debugging, variance estimation for power analysis) and the main series (full factorial plan for Experiments 2–3). ## 8. Theoretical Model: the Okroshka-ness Index

**[Г]** The analysis above is generalized as follows: okroshka is defined not by one ingredient but by a combination of structural features. A formalization is proposed—the **okroshka-ness index (ИО)**—calculated from six axes, each scored from 0 to 1:

**ИО = w₁·O + w₂·Х + w₃·Н + w₄·П + w₅·K + w₆·З**

where:

- **O (liquid base)**—completeness of the base as a culinary carrier: a fermented, extractive acidic liquid (kvass, fermented-milk products) versus neutral water;
- **Х (coldness)**—degree to which cold serving is realized;
- **Н (dicing)**—degree to which a finely diced structure is realized;
- **П (solid filling)**—presence and completeness of the protein-starch solid fraction (meat, fish, egg, potatoes);
- **K (acidity)**—intensity of the acidic component regardless of origin (sensory scale calibrated to the chemical scale—§3.5);
- **З (herbs)**—completeness of the fresh aromatic layer (cucumber, onion, dill, radish, aromatic herbs);
- **w₁…w₆**—weight coefficients; a priori values: w = (0.25; 0.20; 0.15; 0.10; 0.20; 0.10), with base and coldness dominant.

**Note on notation (harmonization with the paired studies).** The letters O (base) and K (acidity) retain the meanings adopted in the ИЩ and ИБ indices [30], [31]; the letter T, used in the paired studies for the technology of slow cooking/sautéing, is not used here—okroshka has no integrating heat treatment, and precisely this absence separates it from borscht in the paired model [31]. The solid filling is denoted П rather than M: the letter M is reserved in the project for the external accompaniment layer (layer M, §3.9), eliminating the collision by the same strategy used to separate P/Ц in the paired study [31]. Carbonation (Г) and pungent seasoning (Тр) are modifiers rather than axes (§§3.3–3.4).

**Operationalization of the scales.** Each axis is scored on a three-point ordinal scale with anchors (harmonized with anchors in the paired models wherever axes overlap [30], [31]):

- O: 0—neutral water; 0.5—acidified water or a diluted/depleted base; 1—a full fermented base (kvass, kefir);
- Х: 0—warm/hot serving (>25 °C); 0.5—room temperature (15–25 °C); 1—cold (4–12 °C, with or without ice). The anchors are derived from culinary practice, but the axis is conceptually continuous (serving temperature is a continuous physical variable); the three-point scale is a working approximation pending data;
- Н: 0—coarse cut (>12 mm) or whole pieces; 0.5—medium (8–12 mm); 1—fine (3–7 mm), with chopped herbs;
- П: 0—no solid filling; 0.5—egg/potato only; 1—full filling (meat/fish + egg ± potato);
- K: 0—no acidic component; 0.5—background acidity; 1—pronounced acidity;
- З: 0—no herbs; 0.5—a single component (only cucumber or only dill); 1—a full fresh set (cucumber + onion + dill ± radish, aromatic herbs).

**Model status.** In the present study, ИО is a **preliminary (heuristic) model of an index of structural okroshka-ness**, not a calibrated measurement instrument. The weights w = (0.25; 0.20; 0.15; 0.10; 0.20; 0.10) are not empirically established coefficients: they form an **a priori model intended for subsequent calibration** on sensory-panel data (§7). The threshold ИО ≥ 0.6 has the same status—an a priori threshold for the pilot model; its empirical value is determined from the ROC curve as the point that optimally separates Experiment 3 panel responses, using the training subset only (split by batches and days), and then evaluated on held-out data. Binary responses from the blinded panel serve as the operational reference; weights are estimated from coefficients of logistic regression of identification on the six axes—a procedure symmetrical to calibration of ИЩ and ИБ in the paired studies [30], [31]. Assignment is two-stage: the necessary filter Х ≥ 0.5 and Н ≥ 0.5, followed by the integral score ИО ≥ 0.6 for the class core (§8.1).

**Collinearity of axes.** As in the paired studies, the axes are correlated through multifunctional carriers: kvass simultaneously shifts O, K, and modifier Г; kefir shifts O, K, and the fat phase; radish shifts Н, З, and Тр (§4.2). Weights estimated by regression on correlated predictors are unstable; calibration therefore includes VIF diagnostics and, where collinearity is substantial, ridge regularization or a transition to latent variables—the same assumption and solution adopted in the paired studies [30], [31].

**Mapping the type code onto the index.** The code Ок = (О, Х, Н, П, К) from §4.4 and the ИО index are two tools with different functions; their relationship is specified explicitly to prevent double counting:

| Code coordinate | Index axis | Nature of mapping |
| --- | --- | --- |
| О (base type, О1–О7) | O (base) | not identical: the code records the *origin* of the base; the index its *structural completeness* (fermentation, extractive body) |
| Х (temperature class, Х1–Х3) | Х (coldness) | direct mapping: temperature class locates the sample on the scale |
| Н (calibre, Н1–Н4) | Н (dicing) | direct mapping with caveat: a grated/puréed mixture (Н4) lies outside the axis scale—see exclusion of gazpacho (§8.1) |
| П (filling type, П1–П5) | П (filling) | partial: the code distinguishes the *kind* of filling; the axis measures *completeness* of the solid fraction |
| К (acid source, К1–К6) | K (acidity) | the source determines the acid profile (coordinate C of vector A [30]); axis K measures *intensity*, not origin |
| — | З (herbs) | an axis without a code coordinate: the green layer varies within all types and is omitted from the code as a constant background of the class |

The type code is a taxonomic instrument (what particular kind of okroshka a sample is), whereas the index is a measurement instrument (how fully the dish realizes the class features)—the same separation of functions as in the paired study [31].

A dish belongs to the class of okroshka if it passes the filter and exceeds the ИО threshold on the aggregate of the axes, without requiring a maximum on any one weighted axis. The key thesis of the model is:

> **[Г] Kvass is not an obligatory element of okroshka, but the historically most efficient means of simultaneously achieving values on axes O and K and modifier Г.** A single beverage supplies acidity, extractive body, sweetness, and carbonation, reducing the need for separate components. This explains its dominance, but not exclusivity: a fermented-milk base reaches the same axes through a different chemical pathway.

**[Г]** The model explains the observed diversity: classic kvass okroshka (maximum on all axes), kefir okroshka (different O and K profile with other factors equal), mineral-water okroshka (lower O with Х, Н, and K preserved), fasting okroshka (lower П), beet okroshka (shift in З plus added sweetness, approaching the cold-beet-soup boundary), and historical fish and “sour-shchi” versions [6].

### 8.1. Class boundaries: core, periphery, and two levels of membership

**Two-stage assignment procedure** (mirroring the paired borscht study [31]):

1. **Necessary filter:** Х ≥ 0.5 and Н ≥ 0.5—cold serving and diced structure. The filter is imposed on **physical** axes; this is the fundamental distinction between okroshka and borscht, whose filter is flavor-based (K ≥ 0.5 and S₁ ≥ 0.5), and shchi, which has no filter at all [30], [31]. A dish that fails the filter cannot be assigned to the class core regardless of the sum ИО. **Caveat on the status of the filter threshold.** The value 0.5 is an a priori setting, not a measured boundary: on the three-point scale, Х ≥ 0.5 formally admits a room-temperature sample (15–25 °C), which is substantively questionable—okroshka at 20–24 °C would hardly be recognized by bearers of the tradition as “proper.” In substantive terms, the filter means “served noticeably colder than room temperature”; the numerical location of the boundary on the continuous temperature axis (and the shape of the transition “okroshka / not okroshka”—abrupt or gradual) is determined experimentally by the temperature morphing of Experiment 4 (the psychometric curve of loss of okroshka-ness). Until calibration, all consequences that depend on the exact threshold value are explicitly treated as a priori.
2. **Integral score:** once the filter is passed, ИО ≥ 0.6 defines the **core** of the class; dishes with ИО 0.4–0.6, as well as dishes that formally fail the filter but belong to a documented family lineage, form the **periphery**.

**General principle for excluding adjacent classes.** The boundary is not defined by a list of “non-okroshkas” but by a general principle: okroshka is a **cold assembled soup consisting of a finely diced mixture in an acidic liquid base**. From this follow particular exclusions:

- shchi and borscht fail filter Х (hot serving) and involve integrating heat treatment, which okroshka lacks [30], [31];
- gazpacho passes Х but fails Н: its puréed structure (Н4) lies outside the three-point Н scale (0/0.5/1 covers only particulate cuts) and therefore automatically excludes the dish from the class; it is a convergent analogue, not a class member;
- classic cold beet soup uses a decoction as the base (different O) and coarse or grated cutting; a chilled beet soup is “borscht that has cooled” in the formulation of the paired study [31], not an assembled okroshka;
- botvinya uses cooked rather than fresh greens, fish filling, and a scalded acidic architecture [8]: genealogically an older form, structurally a neighbor rather than a member;
- tyurya uses bread crumbs as its solid fraction: a genealogical ancestor and a peripheral member through the documented lineage [9];
- tarator combines a fermented-milk base, cucumber, fine chopping or grating, coldness, and sometimes ice [10]: structurally it passes almost the entire filter but belongs to a different (Balkan) tradition, with garlic, walnuts, and oil instead of a meat filling—a canonical case in which the two levels of the model diverge (see below);
- šaltibarščiai uses kefir or buttermilk, cooked beet (puréed or grated rather than diced), cucumber, egg, and cold serving [11]: it is structurally close to kefir beet okroshka, but the beet component makes it primarily a *beet* soup (its similarity to okroshka is limited to the shared fermented-milk base and coldness), while its self-name and tradition assign it to the family of “cold borschts”—a second canonical divergence between the two levels;
- ovdukh is an Azerbaijani cold soup based on katyk with finely chopped cucumber and herbs [28]: the closest foreign analogue of okroshka in fermented-milk base, coldness, and chopping, yet its diced mixture is limited to cucumber and herbs—the filling (Н) and protein component are absent, so under the model the dish passes the filter but does not reach the ИО threshold; it has no genealogy from tyurya and is therefore a convergent neighbor of the fermented-milk branch;
- dovga is an Azerbaijani soup from the same family (yogurt, herbs), but with rice and egg cooked in the soup itself and served either cold or hot [27]: the integrating heat treatment of the assembled components (rice is cooked in the soup rather than added already cooked) and hot serving violate axis Х and the boundary “assembly without integrating heat treatment”; structurally it is farther from okroshka than ovdukh despite sharing the idea of a “fermented-milk soup with herbs”;
- kholodnik is a Belarusian cold soup based on kefir or beet decoction, with cooked (or pickled) beet, cucumber, egg, and herbs [29]: a relative of šaltibarščiai and a second representative of the “beet branch” of convergence; cooked beet gives the dish a beet identity, while its similarity to okroshka is limited to the base, coldness, and the cucumber–egg–herb set.

**Two levels of the model.** As in the paired studies, the following are distinguished:

1. **Structural okroshka-ness**—the degree to which a dish conforms to the model parameters (axes, filter, ИО). It is a continuous, measurable quantity.
2. **Historical-cultural affiliation**—whether the dish belongs to the okroshka family by usage, self-designation, and documented genealogy. This is a binary social fact not derivable from structure alone.

Tarator is structurally “almost okroshka” but historically and culturally is not okroshka; tyurya is structurally peripheral but belongs to the class genealogy. The categorical framework is hybrid, as in the paired borscht study [31]: prototype structure in the sense of Rosch [25] for the core, and family resemblance in the sense of Wittgenstein [24] for the extent of the family, including the periphery by genealogy and self-designation. Historical-cultural affiliation is operationalized by three indicators: the dish’s self-name within the tradition, a documented lineage of recipes, and consensus among bearers of the tradition (measured in Experiment 3 as an external criterion not included in ИО). A testable consequence of the model is that **borderline cases (beet okroshka, tarator, šaltibarščiai) should generate the greatest disagreement and the highest proportion of uncertain responses**, while consensus should be maximal in the core (H6).

### 8.2. Causal scheme of the model

**[Г]** Causal relations among system variables (mirroring the scheme in the paired study [31]):

```javascript
base raw material (wort, milk, water)
        ↓
process (fermentation / acidification) → base type (code О) → acid profile, CO₂, solids
        ↓                                                        ↓
   axis O, axis K, modifier Г ←——— buffer capacity ←—— solids/protein
        ↓
   sensory sourness ←—— temperature (Х), fat phase, sweetness, sour cream (layer M)
        ↓
   perceived typicality → identification (latent membership variable)
        ↑
   dicing (Н) → fresh-cut aroma (З) → “refreshment”
        ↑
   modifier Тр (mustard/horseradish/radish) → trigeminal tone
```

Confounding pathways and their controls are summarized in the matrix in §7: base sweetness, carbonation, fat content, base color, freshness of dicing, and the taster’s habitual base. The two independent inputs into identification—structure (the axes) and genealogy (the historical-cultural affiliation layer)—correspond to the two model levels in §8.1.

### 8.3. Position of the model within the triad: three category configurations

**[Г]** The okroshka-ness index forms a methodological triad with the ИЩ [30] and ИБ [31] models; the models cannot be reduced to one another:

| Parameter | ИЩ (shchi) | ИБ (borscht) | ИО (okroshka) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Constitutive feature | technology (slow cooking), vegetable mass | flavor balance (K, S₁) | physical parameters (Х, Н) |
| Necessary filter | none | flavor: K ≥ 0.5 and S₁ ≥ 0.5 | physical: Х ≥ 0.5 and Н ≥ 0.5 |
| Core threshold | none (threshold-free model) | ИБ ≥ 0.6 | ИО ≥ 0.6 |
| Acidity | optional axis, expected to be weakest | necessary filter, hypothesized strongest predictor | variable axis with replaceable carrier (axis K + code О) |
| Multifunctional carrier | sauerkraut (V, K, T) | beet (Ц¹, S₁, S₂, T) | kvass (O, K, Г) |
| Type code | Щ = (O, V, K, T, N) | Б = (Ц, К, Б, Т, С) | Ок = (О, Х, Н, П, К) |
| Convergent analogue/control | kapuśniak | gazpacho / luosong tang | tarator / šaltibarščiai / gazpacho |

*Note to the table.* In the “multifunctional carrier” row for ИО, carbonation (Г) is listed even though it is a modifier, not an index axis: kvass covers axes O and K and modifier Г, but not axes Х, Н, П, and З; in the ИЩ and ИБ rows only axes are listed. Identically named letters in different models do not necessarily denote the same measurement variable: for example, axis O in ИЩ spans “water → rich broth,” whereas in ИО it spans “neutral water → fermented acidic base”; in joint reading they should be distinguished as O_щ, O_б, and O_ок. A unified metalanguage of features across the triad (shared spaces of temperature, particle structure, heat treatment, acidity, etc.) is the subject of a separate methodological study within the project.

The triad’s hypotheses are mirror-structured and mutually testable: acidity is a weak axis in shchi, a strong predictor in borscht, and a replaceable carrier in okroshka; compensatory architecture is common to all three (P6, H8-borscht, H8-here); cross-class tests (removal of acid from all three dishes; serving okroshka hot) can be conducted within a single restaurant cycle (§7). Full comparison of the three architectures is reserved for a joint methodological paper; the present table provides its working scaffold. The attractive symmetry of the three configurations (no filter / flavor filter / physical filter) is explicitly declared a **hypothesis of the trilogy rather than an established fact**: the possibility that the models have partly been selected to fit the symmetry cannot be excluded, and the experimental program is permitted to break that symmetry.

## 9. Discussion

**9.1. Okroshka as a substitution system.** The central conclusion of the study is the replaceability of the base. In shchi, the dominant multifunctional carrier is the cabbage component (the shchi study specifically stresses that cabbage is an efficient carrier of functions V, K, T but not a necessary element of the class [30]); in borscht, it is the beet axis combined with a flavor filter. In okroshka, by contrast, there is no rigid raw-material core on axis O: the category permits kvass, fermented-milk bases, whey, and acidified water. The functional invariant is not a particular liquid but the conjunction “acidic base + coldness + finely diced solid filling.” This makes okroshka the only dish in the triad whose identity is determined primarily by physical parameters (temperature, calibre, fluidity), rather than by raw-material specificity or a flavor profile. Historically, this is consistent with its genealogy from tyurya—a technique of “crumbling into liquid” not tied to a particular liquid [9].

**9.2. Multimodality of the category with a hypothesis of two principal centers.** The kvass and kefir branches of okroshka differ not only in the source of acidity but in their entire organoleptic architecture: bread kvass contributes CO₂, bread-and-malt aroma, and mild sweetness; kefir contributes a lactic-acid profile, creamy texture, and absence of carbonation. H4 predicts that tasters will recognize both branches as “okroshka,” while the centers of gravity of their prototypes in sensory space will be statistically separated. A third center (the mineral-water/sour-cream branch) is not excluded; hence the formulation “multimodality with a hypothesis of two principal centers,” rather than “bimodality” (tested by mixture modeling, §7). If confirmed, the category “okroshka” would be semantically monolithic but sensorially multimodal—an interesting case for prototype theory [25]: a single verbal label covers two prototypical centers united by a structural (physical), rather than flavor, invariant. This is the mirror image of borscht, whose model assumes one principal prototype center—beet-and-sour [31]; empirical testing of unimodality in the borscht sensory space remains part of that study’s experimental program, and no results were available at the time of the present edition.

**9.3. Okroshka as the most trigeminal dish in the triad.** Neither shchi nor borscht in canonical form contains two independent trigeminal channels. Okroshka in its kvass branch with mustard-horseradish seasoning simultaneously activates: (a) CO₂ chemesthesis through sour taste cells and carbonic anhydrase 4 [17]; (b) TRPA1-sensitive isothiocyanates from mustard and horseradish [20]; and (c) cold thermoreception at 8–12 °C. This triple trigeminal load is a unique feature of the ИО model among the three configurations; modifiers Г and Тр were introduced precisely to represent it.

**9.4. Category boundaries and cross-cultural parallels.** Tarator and šaltibarščiai show that the structural formula “cold assembled soup on an acidic base” arose independently in several culinary traditions [10; 11]. The proposed two-level scheme (structural membership vs historical-cultural identity) allows their kinship with okroshka to be acknowledged without dissolving the category: tarator passes the structural filter (acidic base, coldness, fine cutting) but lies on the periphery at the historical-cultural level (yogurt-walnut profile, no genealogy from tyurya). This illustrates the distinction between a prototypical core and family resemblance across the extent of a category [24; 25].

**9.5. Limitations.** (1) The regulatory ranges (table in §5) are defined by current GOST standards for kvass and kefir [13; 14], but the broader spectrum of fermented-milk bases (cultured sour milk, ayran, buttermilk) is only fragmentarily standardized. (2) All experimental hypotheses H1–H8 have status [П] and require verification under the §7 protocol; until then the weights w₁–w₆ remain a priori. (3) Historical evidence (§1.2) documents the existence and composition of the dish but does not establish sensory mechanisms; the tabular discipline “fact → suggests → does NOT prove” is observed throughout. (4) The model describes domestic and public-catering practice in the Russian tradition; industrial “bottled okroshkas” and regional variants (for example, mineral-water versions) are considered only as points on the substitution ladder.

## 10. Conclusions

1. Okroshka constitutes the third configuration of a culinary category in the research program: unlike the threshold-free prototype model of shchi [30] and the flavor-filtered model of borscht [31], it is described by a physically filtered model with obligatory coldness (Х ≥ 0.5) and dicing (Н ≥ 0.5) axes. [П]
2. The index ИО = w₁·O + w₂·Х + w₃·Н + w₄·П + w₅·K + w₆·З is proposed with a priori weights (0.25; 0.20; 0.15; 0.10; 0.20; 0.10) and threshold ИО ≥ 0.6; carbonation (Г) and trigeminal-seasoning (Тр) modifiers remain outside the index. [П]
3. The liquid base in okroshka is replaceable (kvass ↔ fermented-milk bases ↔ whey ↔ acidified water ↔ sour shchi [6]); the functional invariant of the category is the conjunction “acidic base + coldness + diced structure,” not a particular liquid. [П]
4. Kvass carbonation is not merely a flavor feature but a chemesthetic one: CO₂ is detected by sour taste cells through carbonic anhydrase 4 [17]; this fundamentally distinguishes the kvass branch from the kefir branch and generates the testable hypothesis of category multimodality (H4). [Д—mechanism; П—multimodality]
5. Canonical quantitative ranges of the bases are fixed normatively: kvass acidity 1.5–7.0 acidity units, CO₂ ≥ 0.30%, alcohol ≤ 1.2% (GOST 31494-2012 [13]); kefir acidity 85–130 °T (GOST 31454-2012 [14]); kvass microbiology (*L. casei, Ln. mesenteroides, S. cerevisiae*) has been described experimentally [15]. [Д]
6. The historical sequence (Osipov 1790—Levshin—Molokhovets—Brockhaus and Efron—*Book of Tasty and Healthy Food* 1939) [2; 3; 5; 6; 12] documents continuity of the dish from the late eighteenth century and Soviet canonization of the kvass branch, but does not contain data sufficient for judgments about sensory mechanisms. [Д—composition and terminology; П—reconstruction of sensory profile]
7. A testable experimental program has been formulated (H1–H8; the pilot survey of Experiment 0 and three laboratory experiments with a four-level base design, confounder control, and a traceability matrix), methodologically inherited from the paired studies [30; 31] and open to falsification. [П]
8. The type code Ок = (О, Х, Н, П, К) is introduced with gradations О1–О7, Х1–Х3, Н1–Н4, П1–П5, К1–К6; the similarity metric combines Hamming distance on the code with a typed weighted distance d_f. [П]

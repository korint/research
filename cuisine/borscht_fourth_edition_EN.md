**A. N. Koriakin**
2026
*Research manuscript*

*Interdisciplinary study: food chemistry, fermentation microbiology, sensory analysis, food history*

## Abstract

This study analyzes borscht as a complex culinary system in which flavor and visual characteristics are determined by the interaction of vegetable components, acidity, methods of thermal processing, and regional traditions. Contrary to the common conception of borscht as “beet soup,” the study shows that beet is only one possible carrier of the system’s key functions—pigmentation, sweetness, and the characteristic earthy background. A classification of borscht varieties is proposed according to the source of red color, source of acidity, vegetable composition, and cooking technology. Different forms of beet (table beet, fodder beet, sugar beet, yellow cultivars, and beet greens) are considered as an independent variable affecting the coloristic and flavor characteristics of the dish. The cultural significance of borscht is demonstrated at every level—from imperial culinary canonization in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries and coronation dinners of Russian emperors to the cuisines of emigrant diasporas (Ashkenazi and Far Eastern). The first parametric classification of borscht varieties is proposed along five functional axes (color source, acidity source, base type, beet-treatment method, and vegetable composition), with a formal type code and a similarity metric between varieties. A qualitative theoretical model, the **borscht-ness index (ИБ)**, is formulated to measure the degree of structural similarity between a dish and the prototypical core of the borscht family, while explicitly distinguishing two levels: structural borscht-ness and historical-cultural affiliation with the family. Working parameters of the model are proposed as a calibratable hypothesis and the class boundaries are defined. A program of experimental validation is developed using objective metrics (CIELAB colorimetry, pH measurement, and sensory analysis). The study is theoretical and analytical; empirical validation remains a subject for future research.

**Keywords:** borscht; beet; betalains; betacyanins; acidity; geosmin; culinary classification; sensory analysis; CIELAB; culinary history; diaspora cuisine.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1. Statement of the problem

In popular understanding, borscht is defined as a soup with beet. This definition is also entrenched in reference literature: Encyclopædia Britannica describes borscht as a “beet soup of the Slavic countries” [1]. Culinary reality, however, is broader than the dictionary definition. There are varieties of borscht:

- without beet (green sorrel borscht, white borscht);
- in which beet is replaced by other plants (tomatoes, Swiss chard, beet greens);
- with different types of beet (table, fodder, sugar beet);
- with different sources of acidity (vinegar, lemon juice, kvass, cabbage brine, tomatoes, sorrel);
- with fundamentally different balances of sweetness and acidity (from sweetish variants to sharply sour ones based on beet kvass).

At the same time, bearers of the culinary tradition readily assign all these dishes to the same class. This raises the central research question:

> **What features make a dish borscht?**

### 1.2. Etymology and historical context

It is revealing that the name of the dish itself does not point to beet. According to historical-etymological dictionaries, the word *borscht* originally denoted a plant—common hogweed (*Heracleum sphondylium*), whose edible leaves and stems were used as food [1], [2]. Culinary use of hogweed in the region is documented in primary sources: in *The Herball, or Generall Historie of Plantes* (1597), the English botanist John Gerard recorded that inhabitants of Poland and Lithuania prepared from a decoction of this herb a fermented drink inoculated with sourdough and consumed in place of beer [3]. Research on medieval Polish cuisine confirms that a sour soup was made from fermented hogweed stems and leaves, and that the name of the dish derived from it [4].

Crucially, even at an early stage the term was not tied to one specific ingredient. In nineteenth-century rural Poland, *barszcz* could denote any sour soup—made from barberries, gooseberries, currants, cranberries, plums, or celery [4]. The first dictionary definition of *barszcz* as a “sour soup made from fermented beet” appears only in Jerzy Samuel Bandtkie’s Polish-German dictionary of 1806 [5]. Beet therefore historically displaced hogweed as the carrier of the sour-vegetable profile [1], [4], while hogweed continued to be recorded as a culinary plant in the traditional cuisines of Eastern European peoples into modern times [3], [4], [6].

Documentary evidence from the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries supplements this picture from two directions. In the Novgorod postal-station books (1586–1631), palace gardeners were required to supply “fresh and salted cucumbers and cabbage, onions and garlic, mustard, carrots, radishes, beets for making brines, borscht, and every garden vegetable” [43]: beet is explicitly named as raw material for brine (*rosol*), while “borscht” appears in the same list as a garden crop—that is, still as a plant, consistent with the etymology. At the same time, the earliest documented mention of borscht specifically as a prepared dish dates to 1584: the Danzig merchant agent Martin Gruneweg, traveling through Kyiv from Lviv to Moscow, wrote that the Ruthenians “rarely or never buy borscht, because everyone cooks it at home himself,” since it was their “daily food and drink” [44]. The description “food and drink” points to a liquid dish of the everyday diet, although the source does not describe its recipe. *Domostroi* (mid-sixteenth century) not only advises one to “sow borscht” [40], but also prescribes household fermentation as a normal part of domestic provisioning: “in autumn, salt cabbage and set beet brine” [40]. This is an early written record of beet brine as a prepared acidifier, long before the dictionary canonization of beet borscht in 1806 [5].

**Caveat on evidentiary value.** This historical observation is directly relevant to the present study, but its status must be defined precisely. Etymology and documentary history provide a **historical argument for a broad semantics of the class**, not proof of the structure of the modern dish: the fact that the word once denoted a broader set of sour soups does not automatically imply that modern borscht preserves that structure. The latter is tested not by etymology but by chemistry and experiment (Sections 3 and 7).

An important semantic distinction must also be fixed here; without it, the classification problem remains underdetermined. The study distinguishes **two objects**: (A) the historical *barszcz* family—a broad and fuzzy generic designation for sour soups made from different raw materials [4]; and (B) the modern class “borscht”—a narrower set containing beet or its functional analogues. The parametric model in Sections 4 and 8 primarily describes object B; object A provides the historical frame within which object B emerged through selection of the most effective function carrier. Conflating these two objects is the source of most disputes about “real” borscht.

Thus, already at the etymological and documentary level, beet is seen not to be a constitutive feature of borscht; rather, it became the most successful carrier of the required flavor and color profile.

> **Botanical clarification.** The plant in question is common hogweed (*Heracleum sphondylium* L.; Siberian hogweed, *H. sibiricum*, was also used in Siberia), a native species long consumed in fermented and cooked form. It should not be confused with Sosnowsky’s hogweed (*H. sosnowskyi* Manden.), a giant Caucasian species introduced into the USSR in the 1940s as a silage crop and subsequently becoming a dangerous invasive plant in central Russia: its sap contains high concentrations of furanocoumarins and causes phototoxic skin injury. This species has no relation to the historical dish. It should also be noted that the fresh sap of common hogweed is phototoxic as well (though much less so); traditional consumption always involved fermentation and heat treatment, consistent with the sources: the soup was prepared specifically from fermented hogweed [3], [4].

### 1.3. Cultural significance: from the imperial table to diaspora cuisine

Borscht is a rare example of a dish simultaneously present at every level of the culinary hierarchy, while at each level retaining its identity even as its form changes.

**Imperial integration and the formation of the “Russian cuisine” canon.** The first printed description of borscht in domestic culinary literature appears in Sergei Drukovtsov’s *Culinary Notes* (1779) [7]. As historian Alison Smith shows in *Recipes for Russia: Food and Nationhood under the Tsars*, in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the Russian Empire—a state whose population was predominantly Slavic—integrated dishes from the cuisines of its territories into a single empire-wide culinary canon perceived as “Russian cuisine” [8]. Characteristically, this canonization was integrative rather than exclusive: regional attributions were retained (for example, Elena Molokhovets lists “Little Russian borscht” and “Polish borscht” [9]). The Soviet period continued this line: *The Book of Tasty and Healthy Food* (1939), a deliberate state project for constructing a unified cuisine out of the dishes of the peoples of the country, included 22 borscht recipes [10]. Thus, borscht’s status as an emblematic dish of the Russian culinary tradition is the result of two centuries of imperial and Soviet canonization.

**The imperial table.** Borscht opened the coronation dinner of Nicholas II on 26 May 1896 in the Alexander Hall of the Grand Kremlin Palace: in the menu designed by Viktor Vasnetsov and now held by the Victoria and Albert Museum in London, the first course is listed as “borsch and pepper-pot soup” [11]. According to contemporaries, Alexander III introduced *bortchok*—a lean borscht on porcini broth—into the court kitchen menu [12]. Earlier still, during the Paris peace negotiations of 1815, Alexander I instructed the celebrated French chef Marie-Antoine Carême to serve diplomats a Russian national soup as a demonstrative gesture of cultural identity [13]. The mutual influence of French haute cuisine and the Russian soup tradition produced the aristocratic “bortchok”—a clear ruby-red beet consommé served in bouillon cups [13]; the technique of ennobling borscht with wine is also recorded in domestic culinary classics (Molokhovets’s “Borscht from Baked Beet with Wine”) [9].

**Culinary classics as evidence of variability.** The first edition of Elena Molokhovets’s *A Gift to Young Housewives* (1861) already contains at least seven distinct variants of borscht: Little Russian (with kvass), Polish, baked-beet borscht with wine, celery borscht, lean borscht with *uszka*, crucian-carp borscht, and others [9]. Thus, the variability of borscht as a class of dishes is documented within a single authoritative source more than a century and a half old.

**Borscht in written monuments and literature.** The earliest occurrence of the word “borscht” in a Russian source dates to the mid-sixteenth century and still denotes the plant rather than the dish: *Domostroi* advises that “borscht should be sown by the fence, around the whole garden” [40] (see the etymological analysis in Section 1.2). In V. I. Dahl’s dictionary (1863–1866), the word occurs in several lexical nests—both as a plant (“badran,” “bodran”: “the leaves are used instead of cabbage, and the stalk is eaten raw; Heracleum”) and as a dish (“fermented beet; a kind of shchi, a soup from beet ferment, served with pork or pork fat”). Significantly, Dahl’s *Proverbs of the Russian People* (1862) contains not a single proverb using the word *borscht*: the cultural canonization of the dish was younger than the dish itself [41].

Literature records borscht not as a festive dish but as an everyday one—a marker of domestic prosperity and household competence: a corpus analysis of the Russian National Corpus counts 1,127 occurrences of the word in 608 documents of the main corpus [41]. The chronological chain of references includes N. V. Gogol—“a good borscht with pigeons, which Gapka had cooked” (*The Tale of How Ivan Ivanovich Quarreled with Ivan Nikiforovich*, 1835); G. F. Kvitka-Osnovianenko, who as early as 1840 lists an entire typology of the dish (“borscht with fattened poultry... Sobieski borscht... Skoropadsky borscht... Pechersk fish borscht”—*Pan Khalyavsky*), so that multiplicity of variants was recorded in fiction long before culinary classifications [41]; and I. S. Turgenev, who places borscht alongside shchi as the two basic soups of the Russian table (“How might one find out, dear fellow, what Yenyusha wants for dinner today, shchi or borscht?”—*Fathers and Sons*, 1862). Later examples include A. I. Kuprin (*The Wonderful Doctor*, 1897) and V. Grossman (*Life and Fate*: “borscht with pork on weekdays and holidays alike”) [41]. In a folk Cossack ballad about a mother’s heart, refusal of the table begins specifically with borscht: “From that day the Cossack fell silent and gloomy, ate no borscht, ate no salamata.”

**Diaspora cuisine.** In Ashkenazi Jewish cuisine, borscht became one of the central soups; the widespread English spelling *borscht* derives through the Yiddish spelling באָרשט [14]. Adaptation to the laws of kashrut produced distinct subclasses: a lean vegetarian borscht (without meat broth) or a dairy version with smetana—both avoiding the combination of meat and dairy [14]. Its root-vegetable base made borscht an ideal dish for early-spring Passover, when long-stored beets and cabbage remained available, while dairy borscht with smetana corresponded to the tradition of Shavuot [14]. The diaspora tradition also preserved a regional flavor axis: according to culinary scholar Claudia Roden, Polish Jews preferred sweeter borscht, while Lithuanian Jews preferred it more sour [15], [16]. An extreme case of cultural metonymy is the American **Borscht Belt**: a network of summer resorts in the Catskill Mountains north of New York City that hosted Ashkenazi families from the 1920s through the 1970s (at its peak, up to 500 hotels and bungalow colonies with kosher kitchens). The region took its nickname from the dish, in playful analogy with the “Bible Belt” of the southern United States; the era was immortalized in the film *Dirty Dancing* (1987) [42]. A dish thus gave its name to an entire geographic region—a rare precedent in culinary toponymy.

**The Far Eastern branch.** After 1917, Russian émigrés brought borscht to Harbin and Shanghai. In Shanghai, where as many as 21,000 Russian refugees lived in the 1920s and 1930s and Avenue Joffre, lined with dozens of Russian restaurants, acquired the nickname “Russian Road,” the dish transformed into *luosong tang* (罗宋汤, “Russian soup”). Because beet was not grown in the local climate, it was replaced with cabbage and tomatoes, including industrial tomato paste [17]. *Luosong tang* entered the classics of Shanghai *haipai* (“Western-style”) cuisine, spread throughout China, and later migrated with Shanghai chefs to Hong Kong and onward to North America [17]. Thus, history itself conducted an “experiment in replacing beet with tomatoes”—and the dish retained the identity of “Russian soup” without a single gram of beet.

**Limit of distribution.** Borscht even crossed the Earth’s atmosphere: a tube of borscht prepared for Soviet cosmonauts is exhibited at the Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum in the United States [14].

The overall conclusion of this review is that at every level—the imperial table, the cookbook, religious adaptation, and climate-driven substitution of ingredients—borscht preserves its identity under radical changes in composition. This is further evidence that the identity of the dish is carried not by an ingredient but by structure.

### 1.4. Aim and objectives

**The aim of the study** is to describe borscht as a multifactorial culinary system and construct a model measuring the **degree of structural similarity between a dish and the prototypical core of the borscht family** (rather than searching for a minimal set of necessary ingredients, which does not exist for a historically evolved family).

**Objectives:**

1. Identify and describe the functional parameters of the system (color, acidity, sweetness, base, technology).
2. Construct a classification of borscht varieties according to these parameters.
3. Analyze beet types as an independent variable on the basis of chemical-composition data.
4. Examine functional substitutes for beet, including historical precedents (hogweed, *luosong tang*).
5. Formulate a theoretical model (the “borscht-ness index”).
6. Develop a program for experimental testing with objective metrics.

**Relationship to the paired study.** The present investigation forms a methodological pair with the study of the acid-flavor profile of traditional shchi [45]. Although the framework is shared (functional carriers, a multi-axis prototype model, and a program of falsifiable predictions), the subjects differ substantially: the central problem of borscht is color and the sour-sweet balance, whereas the central problem of shchi is the multidimensionality of the acid profile and fermentation. Common elements of the toolkit (decomposition of the acid profile into components, distinction between chemical and sensory acidity scales, mechanisms controlling perceived acidity, and buffer capacity) are developed in [45] and are adopted here by reference without repetition.

**Status of the study.** This is a theoretical-analytical investigation; no original experiment has yet been conducted (the experimental series in Section 7 is a program). As in the paired study [45], statements are marked by evidentiary strength: **[Д]** — established by sources; **[П]** — transfer of evidence from adjacent fields to borscht (plausible, not tested); **[Г]** — the author’s own hypothesis.

## 2. Materials and Methods

The study is theoretical and analytical, with elements of prospective experimental design.

**Methods:**

1. **Analysis of the scientific literature**—on beet-pigment chemistry (betalains), color stability, the composition of different beet types, acidity of fermented products, sensory science, and historical-culinary sources.
2. **Comparative classification**—systematization of known borscht variants according to the identified parameters, including historical and diaspora branches.
3. **Modeling**—construction of a formalized “borscht-ness index” model.
4. **Design of an experimental series**—development of a reproducible method with objective metrics:
   - **CIELAB colorimetry** (coordinates L*, a*, b*), a standard method for evaluating food color [18];
   - **pH measurement and titratable acidity** of the broth;
   - **sensory analysis** (profiling method, descriptor scaling: acidity, sweetness, earthiness, broth richness).

**Source-critical note.** The sources used in this study fall into three functional layers: (a) peer-reviewed scientific articles—the basis for chemical and sensory claims [18]–[34]; (b) institutional and academic publications (encyclopedias, museums, university monographs, dictionaries, culinary classics)—for historical facts [1]–[17], [35], [36]; and (c) primary documents of living tradition (family recipes, personal communications [37], [38])—used exclusively as ethnographic samples of practice, not as the basis for theoretical claims. The gradation of evidentiary strength is borrowed from the paired study [45] and applied throughout: **[Д]** — established by sources (primarily layers a and b); **[П]** — transfer of evidence from adjacent fields to borscht (plausible, not tested); **[Г]** — the author’s own hypothesis. Historical-cultural data (layer b), as in [45], are used as concordant context rather than as the basis of the conclusions.

## 3. Main Parameters of Borscht as a System

### 3.1. Color

**[Д]** The red color of classic borscht is determined by pigments of the **betalain** group—water-soluble nitrogen-containing compounds occurring in plants of the order Caryophyllales and functionally replacing anthocyanins [19], [20]. Betalains fall into two classes:

- **betacyanins** (red-violet; principally betanin, accounting for 75–95% of beet’s red tone);
- **betaxanthins** (yellow-orange; principally vulgaxanthin I) [21], [22].

**[Д]** The key culinary property of betalains is the **dependence of their stability on pH and temperature**. Betalains are stable within pH 3–7, with betacyanins most stable specifically under acidic conditions [19], [23], [24]. In alkaline media (pH > 8–10), hydrolysis of the aldimine bond occurs with loss of color intensity, and at pH 11 a yellow degradation product—betalamic acid—is formed [23]. Heat treatment also destroys the pigments: betacyanins lose stability above 60 °C and betaxanthins above 40 °C; during boiling, the red color of a betanin solution gradually shifts toward yellow-brown [19], [24]. Boiling red beet for 60, 120, and 180 seconds reduces betacyanin content by 6%, 22%, and 51%, respectively [24].

**[Д → П] Consequence for the borscht system:** the acidic component of the dish performs not only a flavor function but also a **coloristic function**—it stabilizes the red pigment. This is consistent with culinary practice (adding vinegar, lemon juice, or tomatoes while sweating beet in fat) and permits the hypothesis that acidity is a structural rather than optional parameter of the system; rigorous testing of that hypothesis is a task for experiment, not a conclusion from chemistry alone (Section 7, hypotheses H1–H2). Lower pH additionally inhibits polyphenol oxidase and peroxidase, enzymes that degrade betalains [21].

**[П] Empirical technology against pigment degradation.** It is notable that traditional technology empirically arrived at the same solutions dictated by betalain chemistry. Soviet cookbooks of the 1940s and 1950s record a method of “borscht without a fried vegetable base”: vegetables are slowly stewed in their own juices instead of being sautéed, while a separate beet infusion is prepared for color intensity—beet is briefly boiled in broth with vinegar, the liquid is strained, and it is added to the finished borscht at the end of cooking. Both techniques have a precise chemical explanation: a short infusion added after boiling is complete minimizes the pigment’s cumulative heat load (recall that boiling beet for 180 s destroys 51% of betacyanins [24]), while vinegar in the infusion stabilizes the color through pH [23]. What household practice called a “secret of color” is an applied technique for protecting betacyanins, discovered empirically long before laboratory studies. An additional effect is preservation of vegetable structure and natural flavor with lower fat content.

### 3.2. Acidity

**[Д]** Sources of acidity in borscht are diverse:

| Source | Type of acid | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Sauerkraut, cabbage brine | Lactic (product of lactic acid fermentation) | Cabbage fermentation is driven by lactic acid bacteria (*Lactobacillus*, *Pediococcus*, etc.), which accumulate lactic acid and lower pH [25], [26] |
| Vinegar | Acetic | Rapid acidification during beet sautéing |
| Lemon juice, citric acid | Citric | Mentioned by Britannica as standard acidifiers alongside vinegar and kvass [1] |
| Kvass (bread or beet) | Lactic + acetic (fermentation) | Britannica explicitly names kvass as a classic counterbalance to beet sweetness [1]; Molokhovets uses kvass as a borscht base [9] |
| Tomatoes | Citric, malic | Simultaneously a source of acidity, color, and sweetness |
| Sorrel | Oxalic (oxalate) | Basis of green borscht; sorrel’s characteristic sour flavor is due to its high oxalate content [27] |
| Sour berries, fruits | Various organic acids | Regional variants; historically, sour soups of the *barszcz* type used barberries, gooseberries, currants, and plums [4] |

The sources in the table differ not merely by molecule. A detailed decomposition of the acid profile into intensity, acid composition, duration, temporal position, and character—the vector **A = (I, C, D, P, R)**—was proposed in the paired study of shchi [45, Section 3.1] and applies to borscht without modification. The question “are these sources sensorially identical at equal pH?” is no less relevant for borscht than for shchi and is addressed here with the same instrument (Experiment 2, Section 7).

**[Д → П] Beet kvass: microbiology and chemistry of beet fermentation.** Acidity source К1 and technology Т4 rely on beet fermentation and therefore require explicit description, symmetrically with the treatment of cabbage fermentation in the paired study [45, Section 2.1]. Beet kvass is a product of spontaneous (or starter-assisted) lactic acid fermentation of raw beet: a community of lactic acid bacteria (*Lactobacillus*, *Leuconostoc*, etc.) metabolizes root sugars chiefly into lactic and acetic acids, producing a sour profile of biogenic origin, accompanied by fermentation metabolites rather than by a single molecule as in table vinegar. Of particular importance for the borscht system is that fermentation interacts with the pigment profile: the kinetics of betalain degradation in red-beet juice during fermentation and storage have been studied experimentally [21]. Beet kvass is therefore the only acidifier that is simultaneously a carrier and a modifier of color. This makes it a “self-consistent” component of the system: the acid it contributes stabilizes its own pigment (Section 3.1).

### 3.3. Sweetness

**[Д]** The sweetness of borscht is formed by vegetables: beet, carrot, onion, and parsnip. Sugar content in beet varies substantially by type (see Section 5), from moderate levels in table beet to 16–25 g sucrose per 100 g in sugar beet [28], [29]. The “sweetness ↔ acidity” balance is the central flavor axis of the dish: Britannica itself describes kvass, vinegar, or lemon juice as counterweights to beet sweetness [1]. Regional variability of this axis is also recorded ethnographically: sweeter variants were preferred in Polish Jewish tradition and more sour ones in Lithuanian tradition [15], [16].

**Calibration of the balance in household practice.** Significantly, in living culinary tradition the K↔S balance is not rigidly prescribed by the recipe but is **adjusted in real time**. In documented family practice [38], apple-cider vinegar (a milder version of К3) is supplemented with lemon juice (К4) if acidity is insufficient, while sugar is added “to taste” at the end of cooking (roughly a small spoonful per pot). The cook thus acts as a feedback regulator of the balance: acidity and sweetness are adjusted iteratively until the target profile is reached. **[Г]** This supports the status of axes K and S as independent and actively controlled variables of the system rather than recipe constants. The same practice records the importance of base quality (“much depends on the broth”—the deliberate choice of brisket for the broth [38]) and a finishing aromatic dressing of garlic, a technique also found in the family tradition documented in [37]. In the terminology of the paired shchi study [45, Section 4.5], this calibration is a particular case of the four mechanisms controlling perceived acidity (dilution, masking, neutralization, compensation): adjustment with vinegar and lemon juice is compensation along the acidity axis, while adding sugar is masking; the framework transfers fully to borscht.

### 3.4. Earthy note (background axis)

**[Д]** The characteristic “earthy” note of beet is caused by **geosmin** (trans-1,10-dimethyl-trans-9-decalol), a volatile compound produced by the root crop; its content depends on cultivar [30], [31]. Human olfaction is extremely sensitive to geosmin (thresholds in the low nanogram-per-liter range), while its concentration in the peel is approximately six times higher than in the flesh [30]. **[Д → П]** Crucially for the borscht system, **geosmin reacts with acid to form a non-odorous product (argosmin)** [30]: the acidic component of the dish not only preserves color but also suppresses the earthy note. This is a second chemical argument for the structural role of acidity.

### 3.5. Base

**[Д]** The liquid base of borscht varies: meat broth (beef, pork, poultry), fish broth (Molokhovets gives a crucian-carp borscht [9]), mushroom broth (a Polish fasting variant uses wild-mushroom stock [1]; Alexander III’s court *bortchok* used porcini broth [12]), vegetable stock, or a kefir-beet decoction (cold borscht varieties). **[Г]** The base determines richness/body of broth and the umami background (umami—the fifth basic taste, “savory” or “brothy”; its carriers are glutamate and certain nucleotides in meat, mushrooms, and tomatoes; see the paired study [45, Section 2.3] for detail), but it does not carry a specifically “borscht” function; that role is common to the broader class of rich dressed soups.

## 4. Classification of Borscht Varieties

### 4.1. Existing approaches and the classification problem

**[Д]** A review of the sources shows that no systematic classification specifically of borscht has previously existed. Available approaches reduce to two types:

1. **Classification of soups in general** (educational and industry literature on institutional food technology): soups are divided by serving temperature (hot / cold), liquid base (broths, decoctions, milk, kvass, fermented-milk products), and cooking method (dressed, puréed, clear) [35]. In such schemes, borscht is merely one item within the group of dressed soups; the internal structure of the borscht class is not described.
2. **Enumerative lists** (recipe collections and encyclopedias): borscht variants are listed by name—“Ukrainian,” “Moscow,” “Siberian,” “Naval,” “Polish,” and so forth [36]—without identifying the system of features that generates this diversity.

In other words, no classification of borscht **by functional parameters**, as opposed to an administrative classification “by country or name,” has been proposed in the literature. The scientific novelty of the present study lies precisely in how knowledge is organized: not in the “discovery” of new facts about borscht, but in constructing a new system for describing it—a parametric classification, a formal type code, and a metric of similarity among varieties.

### 4.2. Parametric classification: five functional axes

**[Г]** It is proposed that borscht varieties be classified not by country of origin but by five relatively autonomous functional parameters, each taking discrete values. The phrase “relatively autonomous” is used precisely: the axes are not statistically independent variables—one carrier may shift several axes at once (beet: color, sweetness, and earthiness; tomatoes: color, acidity, and sweetness; acid: both flavor and pigment stability). The axes are independent only in the sense that each describes a separate function of the system and can be fulfilled by different carriers; their interaction through multifunctional carriers is exactly what the model seeks to describe (see the functional matrix below).

**Axis Ц — source of color:**
- Ц1 — table beet (betacyanins);
- Ц2 — other carriers of beet pigments (fodder or sugar beet, yellow cultivars, beet greens, Swiss chard);
- Ц3 — tomatoes (lycopene);
- Ц4 — green leafy vegetables (chlorophyll: sorrel, nettle, spinach);
- Ц0 — red pigment absent (white borscht varieties).

**Axis К — source of acidity:**
- К1 — kvass (bread or beet);
- К2 — sauerkraut, cabbage brine (lactic acid fermentation);
- К3 — vinegar (table, apple-cider);
- К4 — lemon juice, citric acid;
- К5 — tomatoes;
- К6 — sorrel (oxalates);
- К7 — fermented-milk base (kefir, soured milk);
- К8 — sour berries and fruits (historical variants—barberry, gooseberry [4]; living practice—sour apples, prunes).

**Axis Б — type of liquid base:**
- Б1 — meat broth;
- Б2 — fish broth;
- Б3 — mushroom broth;
- Б4 — vegetable stock (fasting/lean);
- Б5 — fermented-milk base.

**Axis Т — method of treating beet (or its functional substitute):**
- Т1 — light sautéing with acid;
- Т2 — baking (Molokhovets’s “baked-beet” variant [9]);
- Т3 — boiling directly in the broth;
- Т4 — separate beet kvass/decoction subsequently combined with the base;
- Т5 — slow stewing without sautéing (vegetables in their own juices, including with beet infusion added at the end—the method of Soviet recipes of the 1940s–1950s);
- Т0 — not applicable.

The value Т0 requires clarification: absence of beet is not a “method of treatment” but absence of the object to be treated. Thus Т0 means “axis not applicable”: the corresponding functions are then fulfilled by carriers on other axes (sorrel—Ц4·К6, tomatoes—Ц3·К5), and the substitution itself is recorded by those axes rather than by T. This preserves homogeneity of the coordinate: all nonzero T values describe actual technological operations.

**Axis С — vegetable composition and garnish structure:**
- С1 — full classic set (cabbage, potato, carrot, onion);
- С2 — reduced set;
- С3 — expanded set (beans, bell pepper, roots);
- С4 — without cabbage;
- С5 — without garnish (clear serving).

**Rationale for the choice and granularity of axes.** The set of axes is not arbitrary: the five parameters correspond to the five functional components of the system identified in Section 3 (color, acidity, base, processing technology, vegetable composition). Potential “sixth axes” are deliberately not introduced for two reasons. First, serving temperature is not an independent variable: it is tightly coupled to the type of base and treatment (fermented-milk base Б5 and separate beet infusion Т4 together imply cold serving), and separating it would create a redundant coordinate. Second, aromatics (garlic, lard, herbs) and textural techniques (flour sauté, geometry of vegetable cutting) do not change membership in the class and are therefore assigned to the modifier layer (Section 4.4): borscht does not cease to be borscht without garlic, but it does cease to be borscht without the sour-sweet axis (see Section 8.1).

The granularity of values within the axes is defined by **functional substitutability**: two values are distinguished if substituting one for the other perceptibly changes the flavor or color profile. Vinegar and lemon juice are separated (К3 and К4) because they differ in aroma and behavior during processing; berries and fruits are combined (К8) because they perform the same acidifying function and were historically interchangeable [4].

Finally, functional autonomy of the axes does not mean that one carrier cannot satisfy several axes (tomatoes—Ц3 and К5, sorrel—Ц4 and К6): the code records functions, not ingredients. Correlation among carriers is not a defect of the model but its principal measurable content: it is precisely beet’s multifunctionality that explains its dominance (Section 8).

**[Г] Functional matrix of components.** The above can be represented as a “component → functions” matrix using a conventional three-point scale for contribution intensity:

| Component | Color | Acid | Sweetness | Earthiness | Structure/body |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Table beet | +++ | + | +++ | +++ | ++ |
| Tomatoes | +++ | ++ | ++ | – | ++ |
| Sorrel | + (green) | +++ | – | – | + |
| Kvass (bread/beet) | – | +++ | + | + | + |
| Cabbage (including sauerkraut) | – | + | + | – | +++ |
| Carrot | + | – | ++ | – | ++ |

**[Г]** The matrix makes the central property of the system visually explicit—**functional redundancy**: almost every function can be fulfilled by several components, while key components are multifunctional. This is why borscht is robust as a system: it is preserved not because one specific ingredient is preserved, but because the set of functions is preserved. Substituting one carrier for another (beet → tomatoes, vinegar → kvass) redistributes functions among components while preserving the aggregate profile. Beet historically dominates precisely as the most “economical” carrier: one root vegetable fills four columns of the matrix at once.

*Status of the matrix.* The values shown are expert estimates (on a conventional three-point scale), not measurements: the matrix is a hypothesis about the distribution of functions and must be tested. After the experimental series (Section 7), it should be recalculated from data: color by pigment concentration and CIELAB coordinates; acid by pH/TA and acid profile; sweetness by sugar content and sensory assessment; earthiness by geosmin (GC–MS). The three-point scale is used before calibration only as a visual summary and does not enter model calculations.

### 4.3. Formal type code

The five-dimensional classification allows any borscht to be represented by a compact **type code** Б = (Ц, К, Б, Т, С). Coordinate **Ц** of the code records the **source of pigment** (beet / tomatoes / absent)—in the terminology of Section 8, this is **Ц²**, whereas **Ц¹** (observed dish color, CIELAB) is an axis of the ИБ index. Superscripts are introduced to distinguish the origin of color from its measured magnitude and are used consistently below (see the notation note in Section 8). The code allows composite coordinate values where a variant uses multiple carriers of the same function (for example, К3+К5—vinegar and tomatoes simultaneously). Examples:

| Variant | Type code |

| Molokhovets’s “Borscht from baked beet with wine” [9] | Ц1·К3·Б1·Т2·С1 |
| Lean Ashkenazi borscht [14] | Ц1·К4·Б4·Т1·С1 |

### 4.4. Technological modifiers and serving ritual

Outside the five axes lies a layer of **technological modifiers**—techniques that do not constitute independent axes but alter texture and aroma within a single code: flour sauté (density and “silkiness” of the broth), geometry of vegetable cutting (the Naval standard of regular cubes [36]), a finishing paste of ground lard with garlic and herbs (independently documented in [37] and in generalized recipe practice), and finishing garlic [38]. **Serving ritual** is distinguished separately as an element of dish identity: *pampushky* with garlic sauce; rye (Borodinsky) bread, including bread rubbed with garlic or served with lard; smetana; fresh herbs; lard/schmalz; hot pepper. The composition of the accompaniment is not semantically accidental: starchy bread and *pampushky* form a neutral counterpoint to the sour-sweet axis, lard and garlic provide fatty and aromatic accents, while smetana acts as a final regulator of perceived acidity (the masking mechanism in the classification of the paired study [45, Section 4.5]). Crucially, this layer lies **outside** the structural code and outside axis T of the borscht-ness index (Section 8): technology is an internal property of the cooking process, whereas serving is an external cultural attribute appearing after that process. In model terms, serving ritual forms a separate layer of cultural modifiers (M): it affects perception and status of the dish but not its structural borscht-ness. This distinction also resolves heterogeneity in axis T: cold borscht variants differ from hot ones not by “different serving within technology,” but by base and treatment type (Б5, Т4), with serving ritual remaining a common modifier M.

### 4.5. Similarity metric among varieties

The type code turns the set of borscht varieties into a finite-dimensional discrete space in which a **distance between varieties** can naturally be defined as the number of mismatched coordinates (Hamming distance):

**d(Бᵢ, Бⱼ) = Σ [coordinate differs], 0 ≤ d ≤ 5**

Examples: d(classic, Little Russian) = 1 (only the acidity source differs); d(classic, *luosong tang*) = 3 (color, acidity, treatment); d(classic, green) = 3; d(green, white) = 3.

**Composite values and missing data.** Two practical situations require explicit rules. (1) A coordinate may have a composite value (К3+К5—vinegar and tomatoes simultaneously): in that case the coordinate contribution is not binary but calculated as the Jaccard distance between sets of values, d_j = 1 − |A ∩ B| / |A ∪ B|; thus d(К3, К3+К5) = 1/2, whereas d(К3, К5) = 1. (2) If classification on an axis is ambiguous or data are unavailable, that coordinate is excluded from the sum and the result is normalized by the number of comparable axes; if several classifications of one sample are plausible, the distance is given as an interval between the minimum and maximum values rather than as a point. Both rules preserve interpretability of the unweighted metric and transfer directly to the weighted form d_w.

**Limitation and development of the metric.** The basic (unweighted) form does not distinguish the importance of discrepancies on different axes: a color discrepancy (Ц1 vs Ц4—beet vs sorrel) and a discrepancy in garnish composition each contribute d = 1, although these differences are not culinarily equivalent. As a development, a **weighted distance** d_w = Σ w_j·[coordinate j differs] is proposed, where axis weights w_j are inherited from calibration of the borscht-ness index (Section 8): discrepancies on the axes most important to the class (K, S) will “cost” more than discrepancies on optional axes (Ц). Finally, when moving from discrete codes to continuous measurements (CIELAB, pH, titratable acidity—Section 7), the metric naturally generalizes to a weighted Euclidean distance d = √Σ w_j·(x_j − y_j)². Thus, the classification permits three levels of precision—qualitative (d), weighted (d_w), and quantitative (Euclidean)—and can become progressively more rigorous as measurement data accumulate. Before calibration, weighted forms remain programmatic and the unweighted form is retained as a conservative lower-bound measure of similarity.

**Status of Hamming distance as an explicit baseline.** Unweighted Hamming distance occupies the **baseline** position in the hierarchy of metrics: it is the simplest reasonable metric against which any real gain from added complexity is evaluated. Development proceeds along two dimensions simultaneously: weighting (w_j from calibration of ИБ) and typing of element-wise distances. The code coordinates are heterogeneous, so the substantively appropriate metric is the typed sum **d_f(Бᵢ, Бⱼ) = Σⱼ wⱼ·dⱼ(Бᵢⱼ, Бⱼⱼ)**, where for nominal coordinates (acidity source К, garnish composition С), dⱼ is a binary mismatch or Jaccard distance for composites; for ordered scales (base type Б, treatment method Т), it is the normalized ordinal difference in positions; and for coordinate Ц once measurements are available, it is distance in CIELAB space (ΔE between observed colors, axis Ц¹ in Section 8), rather than disagreement between pigment-source codes (Ц²). The criterion for accepting the more complex metric is superiority over the Hamming baseline in predicting sensory and identification similarity among samples (Experiment 3): if the typed weighted metric does not predict panel responses better than d, the complication is rejected.

The metric provides a quantitative tool for comparative analysis: clustering regional variants, constructing a “tree” of borscht varieties, and formally defining class boundaries (variants with d ≤ 2 from the classic reference form its immediate neighborhood). This moves discussion of “real” and “not real” borscht from the plane of opinion to the plane of measurement.

The type code and the borscht-ness index (Section 8) are complementary: the code describes *what kind* of borscht a sample is, while the index describes *how fully* it realizes the features of the class.

The intersection of the axes generates dozens of stable configurations, consistent with the historical evidence: *barszcz* served as a generic designation for an entire family of sour soups made from very different raw materials [4]; the dictionary fixation of the beet variant as the norm dates only to the early nineteenth century [5]; and cookbooks from the second half of the eighteenth century onward [7], [9] already contained numerous stable variants of the dish.

## 5. Beet Types as an Independent Variable

All cultivated forms of beet belong to the single species *Beta vulgaris* L., yet their chemical composition differs radically, making the type of raw material a full-fledged experimental variable.

### 5.1. Table beet

**[Д]** The baseline variant. It is characterized by a high concentration of betacyanins: in dark-red (burgundy) cultivars, betalain content averages 141.85 mg/100 g fresh weight (range 64.5–218.8 mg/100 g), whereas yellow cultivars contain only about 24% of this level [22]. The principal pigments of burgundy cultivars are betanin (59%) and isobetanin (23%) [22]. Table beet provides saturated color, moderate sweetness, and a pronounced earthy note (with cultivar-dependent variability in geosmin [31]).

### 5.2. Fodder beet

**[П]** Large roots with higher water content, lower sweetness, and lower pigment density. The research question is: how does borscht change when table beet is replaced by fodder beet? Expected effects include a paler color (lower betacyanin concentration per unit mass), the need to intensify acidic and sweet components, and a longer heat-treatment time—which in turn accelerates thermal degradation of pigment [24] and may produce a “rusty” hue.

### 5.3. Sugar beet

**[Д]** Contains 16–25 g sucrose per 100 g [28], [29] (an average of 18.8 g/100 g in fresh roots, rising to 25.2 g/100 g during uncovered storage [28]). **[П]** The use of sugar beet radically shifts the flavor balance toward sweetness and requires proportional strengthening of the acidic component. Deliberate control of the sweetness axis is not a modern invention: classic technology explicitly regulates the “sweetness–acidity” balance, including acidification with kvass, vinegar, or lemon juice [1].

### 5.4. Yellow cultivars of table beet

**[Д]** A contrasting case: the pigment profile shifts from betacyanins toward betaxanthins (yellow-orange range); in yellow cultivars, vulgaxanthin IV dominates, accounting for 80% of betaxanthins [22]. **[Г]** Borscht made with yellow beet is a natural experiment in replacing the pigment class: the dish preserves “beet” sweetness and texture but loses its red color, allowing the significance of color for identification as borscht to be tested.

### 5.5. Beet greens

**[Д]** Young beet leaves are a historically early component (“borscht from beet tops”). Contemporary data show that *B. vulgaris* leaves contain significant quantities of betaxanthins (13.4–20.4 mg/kg fresh weight depending on cultivar) [32], and that in aggregate chemical profile, antioxidant activity, and antimicrobial activity the leaves exceed the corresponding roots [33]. **[Д → П]** At the same time, most root pigments are concentrated in the peel [22]—an important technological consequence, because peeling beet reduces both pigmentation and geosmin content, which is likewise concentrated in the peel [30].

## 6. Functional Substitutes for Beet

**[Г]** The central idea is to search not for “another beet,” but for plants that perform **the same function** in the system (color, acidity, sweetness, earthiness).

### 6.1. Swiss chard (leaf beet, *Beta vulgaris* subsp. *cicla*)

**[Д]** The closest relative of table beet; its leaves and petioles are eaten. Chard leaves contain pigments of **both betalain classes**—betacyanins (betanin, isobetanin) and betaxanthins [33], [34], with total betaxanthin content in chard leaves (up to 107 mg/kg fresh weight) substantially higher than in leaves of root-beet cultivars [32]. Petioles of colored cultivars can impart a pink-red tone to broth. **[Д → П]** Swiss chard is the only substitute that preserves “beet chemistry” in the strict sense.

### 6.2. Sorrel (*Rumex acetosa*)

**[Д]** Sorrel does not produce red color but creates characteristic acidity—the basis of green borscht. Its sour flavor is due to high oxalate content (273–954 mg total oxalates per 100 g fresh leaves) [27]. Notably, sorrel soup retains its characteristic sour taste even after cooking and dilution, while adding smetana (the classic serving of green borscht) reduces the fraction of soluble oxalates by binding them with calcium [27]. **[Г]** Sorrel borscht demonstrates that the acidic function is more fundamental to the system than the pigment function.

### 6.3. Tomatoes

**[Д → П]** Tomatoes functionally replace part of beet’s role on three axes at once: they provide red color (lycopene), acidity (citric and malic acids), and sweetness. In a number of regional practices, a sautéed tomato base completely or partially displaces beet. Tomatoes form a natural “bridge” between beet and beet-free borscht.

**[Д]** The most striking experiment in replacing beet with tomatoes was conducted by history itself: in Shanghai *luosong tang*, which developed from the borscht of Russian émigrés, beet was completely replaced by tomatoes and tomato paste because the local climate did not permit its cultivation; nevertheless, the dish retained both its name (“Russian soup”) and its structure (beef, cabbage, potatoes, sour-sweet red broth) [17].

### 6.4. Historical precedent: hogweed

**[Д]** The original function carrier of the dish was hogweed (*Heracleum sphondylium*), whose fermented stems and leaves supplied the acidity from which the name arose [1]–[4]. **[Д → П]** The historical change of carrier (hogweed → beet) demonstrates that the borscht system permits substitution of a central ingredient while preserving dish identity—a direct precedent for modern substitutes.

## 7. Experimental Validation Program

To test the theoretical propositions, a series of controlled-variable experiments is proposed. This section is a research program rather than a report of completed studies.

**[Г] Testable hypotheses of the model.** The model developed in Sections 4 and 8 is formulated predictively: it specifies what should occur when components are changed under controlled conditions. Eight key hypotheses are proposed:

- **H1.** Acidity is the strongest single predictor of identifying a dish as borscht: removing the acidic component reduces the proportion of “borscht” identifications more strongly than removing any other single feature.
- **H2.** Removal of beet alone should not sharply reduce identification if axes K, S, and T are preserved (functional substitutability of the carrier).
- **H3.** Altering red color while preserving K/S/T will have a smaller effect on identification than disrupting the sour-sweet axis.
- **H4.** Structural distance from the classic reference (d_w, Section 4.5) will correlate negatively with the probability that the dish is recognized as borscht.
- **H5.** Peripheral traditional samples (white borscht, *barszcz czysty*, *luosong tang*) will show high cultural status (name recognition) while having lower structural borscht-ness and maximum dispersion of responses—distinguishing the periphery from the core.
- **H6 (cross-class).** Removing the acidic component reduces borscht identification more strongly than the analogous removal reduces shchi identification in the paired study [45]: in borscht, acidity is a necessary filter; in shchi, it is an optional axis. The asymmetry of threshold logic between the two models should therefore appear as a difference in the magnitude of the drop. This is a direct joint test of the pair of models, not two parallel hypotheses.
- **H7.** Color versus pigment origin: when observed redness is equalized (equal a* in CIELAB, axis Ц¹), samples pigmented by beet and by tomatoes will not differ in identification by a blinded panel in a visually blinded subseries; if a difference persists when color is hidden, it is carried by non-pigment correlates of the carrier (taste, aroma), not by “beet-ness” as such. The hypothesis separates two coordinates conflated in earlier versions of the model: observed color (Ц¹) and pigment source (Ц²) (Section 8).
- **H8.** Compensatory architecture: weakening one ИБ axis is partly compensated by strengthening another while identification is preserved (for example, lowering Ц while strengthening S and T—white borscht remains borscht for tradition bearers). Falsification: identification is additive across axes, with no significant interactions. The hypothesis is symmetric to prediction P6 of the paired study [45] and is tested by the same procedure (comparison of models with and without axis-interaction terms).

**General design requirements.** For all experiments in the series: at least **n = 5 independent preparations** of each variant (the experimental unit is a separate cooking batch, not an individual taster); blind sample coding; randomized serving order; control of serving temperature, vegetable mass, cooking duration, salt concentration, cutting geometry, volume, and serving vessel. When comparing raw-material types, the principle used to standardize the experimental unit must be fixed in advance—equal raw-material mass or equal content of a target marker (for example, betalains). Mixing these principles within one series is prohibited because it turns one factor into several. The protocol is aligned with the experimental program of the paired shchi study [45]; divergences (n = 5 batches rather than three; absence of a single serving temperature because cold borscht variants make the shchi standard of 65 ± 2 °C inapplicable; an additional survey-based Experiment 0) reflect the specifics of the object, while panel questions in Experiment 3 have been made compatible (binary identification + graded similarity).

**Temperature as a covariate.** Because a single serving temperature is impossible for borscht (cold variants exist), temperature is included in the statistical models as a covariate, and all comparisons are made within temperature class. In the joint two-week cycle [45], hot shchi (65 ± 2 °C) and cold borscht (≈4 °C) are not directly compared; if cross-temperature conclusions are needed, a separate temperature-controlled subexperiment is planned.

**Blinding and carryover effects.** Color is a strong sensory cue that is difficult to blind. To estimate the contribution of the visual channel to identification, a control subseries is planned using opaque cups (or monochromatic lighting that masks coloration). Carryover between soup samples is important: between tastings, panelists receive water and neutral bread with a fixed interval of at least 2 minutes; with many variants, the panel is divided into balanced incomplete blocks to limit fatigue.

**Matrix of confounding variables.** Each confounder in the causal scheme (Section 8.2) is paired with an explicit control measure:

| Confounding variable | Mechanism of bias | Control measure |
|---|---|---|
| Serving temperature | changes perception of acidity, sweetness, and volatiles | temperature as covariate; comparisons within temperature class |
| Visual channel | red color creates an expectation of “borscht-ness” | subseries in opaque vessels / monochromatic lighting; test H7 |
| Serving order, fatigue | contrast and adaptation effects | balanced randomization; incomplete blocks; pauses ≥ 2 min |
| Matrix (salt, fat, dry matter) | suppresses acidity and sweetness without changing acid composition | recipe standardization; Level Г of Experiment 2 (normalization for NaCl and dry matter) |
| Batch / day | between-batch variation, common raw-material lot | random effects (1 \| batch), (1 \| day); n = 5 batches |
| Taster phenotype | individual strength of taste suppression | random effect (1 \| taster); repeated ratings of a subset of samples |
| Taster’s cultural experience | shifts identification without changing sensory properties | experience questionnaire; Experiment 0 as external calibration cross-section |

**Pilot and main series.** The series is conducted in two stages: a pilot (panel of 8–12 tasters—a typical size for descriptive sensory analysis) refines the protocol and estimates actual effect sizes; the main series is planned from the pilot results, with the number of batches and panel size determined by power analysis and with optional expansion through restaurant-customer tasting under informed consent (which does not replace the trained panel).

**Statistical plan.** Specified in advance, before the series: CIELAB colorimetry, pH, and titratable acidity—one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test when assumptions are violated; sensory ratings—a mixed-effects model of the form “rating ~ variant + serving order + temperature + (1 | taster) + (1 | batch)”; for batches prepared on different days, the random effect (1 | day) is added because batches from the same day are not fully independent (common raw-material lot, common kitchen regime); identification (“borscht / not borscht”)—logistic regression on the model axes. The number of independent batches (n = 5) and panel size are justified by power analysis for a prespecified minimum meaningful effect size; multiple comparisons (repeat colorimetry at 24/48 h, series of variants) use Holm correction or FDR control. On completion of the series, open data are to be published: raw sensory ratings, CIELAB/pH/TA measurements, and recipe specifications. The statistical plan is aligned with the paired study [45] and identical to it in its general components.

**Mapping theoretical features to measurable indicators:**

| Theoretical feature | Measurable indicator |
|---|---|
| Redness | CIELAB a* [18] |
| Lightness | CIELAB L* [18] |
| Yellowness (yellow cultivars) | CIELAB b* [18] |
| Chemical acidity | pH; titratable acidity |
| Buffer capacity | volume of alkali required to reach a specified pH (method from [45]) |
| Sensory acidity | perceived intensity (profiling method) |
| Sweetness (S₁) | sugar content; sensory rating |
| Earthiness (S₂) | geosmin (GC–MS); sensory rating |
| “Borscht-ness” | proportion of blind-panel identifications as borscht |

Importantly, **chemical and sensory acidity are different quantities**: two products with the same pH may have different titratable acidity and different perceived sourness. Therefore axis K is measured with two indicators in parallel, and conclusions about the flavor role of acidity are based on the sensory rather than solely the instrumental channel. The distinction between chemical and sensory acidity scales is developed in detail in the paired study [45, Section 3.2] and is adopted here without repetition. Borscht, like shchi, is a buffered system (broth proteins, organic acids, potato starch), so buffer capacity is included among the measurements even where it is not the central variable.

### Experiment 0. Pilot survey on necessary features

Before the experiments proper, a survey is conducted (at least 30 participants who practice home or professional cooking): respondents rate a list of features (beet, cabbage, meat broth, acid, sweetness, red color, potato, carrot, smetana, garlic, sautéing) on the scale “necessary / desirable / optional / irrelevant.” The result provides an **empirical baseline of human classification**, against which the functional model is then compared: agreement between the ranking of features and model weights constitutes the model’s first external validation, while discrepancies indicate the direction in which the weights should be calibrated.

### Experiment 1. Beet type as a variable

The recipe and technology are identical; only the beet type differs: table beet (burgundy cultivar) / fodder beet / sugar beet / yellow table beet.

**Metrics:** broth color in CIELAB coordinates (L*, a*, b*) [18]; pH of the finished dish; sensory evaluation (sweetness, acidity, earthiness, broth richness—profiling method, 10-point scales), including a **temporal perception profile** (sequence: acidity at the beginning of the sip → beet-meat sweetness in the middle → spicy finish) as a target descriptor of the classic profile.

**[П/Г] Hypotheses:** (a) fodder beet will yield significantly lower a* values (less red broth); (b) sugar beet will require a higher dose of acidifier to maintain balance; (c) yellow beet will shift color along the b* axis without losing the “beet” flavor profile.

**Methodological caveat:** “beet type” is not a single chemical variable but a **multifactor treatment defined by raw-material type** (pigments, sugars, water, fiber, geosmin, and softening kinetics all change simultaneously). The experiment is therefore interpreted as a comparison of technological cultivar-types rather than isolation of the pigment factor; isolating the latter requires standardization by betalain content (see general design requirements).

### Experiment 2. Acidity source as a variable (four-level design)

The design is symmetric to Experiment 2 of the paired study [45] and separates the effects of molecule, profile, metabolites, and matrix:

- **Level А — real acidifiers:** identical composition; only the acidifier differs: vinegar / lemon juice / cabbage brine / tomatoes / beet kvass.
- **Level Б — individual pure acids:** the same base + pure acetic / citric / lactic acid.
- **Level В — mixtures of pure acids reproducing the profile:** for each Level А acidifier, a mixture of pure acids reproducing its profile C according to HPLC data (for example, lactic + acetic for cabbage brine; citric + malic for tomatoes), without other metabolites or accompanying substances.
- **Level Г — real product in a standardized matrix:** the same acidifier introduced into a matrix normalized for NaCl and dry-matter content.

**Contrast logic:** Б→В estimates the effect of the *acid profile*; В→Г the effect of *fermentation metabolites and accompanying substances*; Г→А the effect of the *matrix*. Equalization by pH and titratable acidity is performed within each level (subseries modeled on [45]).

**Metrics:** pH and titratable acidity; color stability during storage (repeat CIELAB colorimetry after 24 and 48 h at 4 °C, following methods used in betalain-degradation studies [21]); sensory analysis using vector A = (I, C, D, P, R) [45].

**[П] Hypothesis:** variants with fermented acidifiers (brine, kvass) will receive higher sensory ratings because of their more complex flavor profile, by analogy with evidence of improved sensory ratings of sauerkraut under directed lactic acid fermentation [26]. In logic, the hypothesis coincides with prediction P3 of the paired shchi study [45] (fermented carriers versus pure acids at equal intensity); sensory data are collected using vector A = (I, C, D, P, R) [45], making the two experimental series directly comparable.

### Experiment 3. Borscht without beet (central test of the model)

This is the key experiment of the series: it directly tests the entire theoretical construction. The series includes variants compensating for the absence of beet: (a) tomatoes + increased carrot proportion (the *luosong tang* model [17]); (b) Swiss chard; (c) beet greens; (d) control—green sorrel borscht; (e) negative controls—variants with disrupted axis K (no acidifier) and disrupted axis Ц¹ (no red pigment) while the other axes are preserved; (f) cross-dish negative control—shchi (fresh and sour): a dish with acidity but without the sweet-earthy root-vegetable axis. Inclusion of shchi is symmetric to inclusion of borscht in Experiment 3 of the paired shchi study [45] and directly tests H4 at the class boundary. The set additionally includes: (g) a convergent analogue—gazpacho (or an Italian tomato soup with carrot and onion), formally close along axes Ц3, К5, S but with a different genealogy. This directly tests the genealogical criterion of Section 8.1: the model predicts rejection of gazpacho by tradition bearers despite structurally high ИБ, which would support the two-level nature of the model (structure measures degree of membership within the family; genealogy separates the family from convergent analogues); (h) an acid-removal series—classic borscht without acidifier, with the drop in identification compared with the analogous shchi series from the first week of the cycle [45], providing a cross-class test of H6 on the asymmetry of threshold logic; and (i) a **fully beet-free functional double**—a strengthened version of variants (b)–(c): Swiss chard and beet greens are chemically too close to beet (the same betalains), so the series includes a sample constructed without any beet-derived raw material, with four channels artificially matched to the prototype—color (red pigment of non-beet origin, matched by CIELAB a*), sweetness (sugar adjusted to prototype S₁), acidity (adjusted to prototype pH/TA), and earthy note (food-grade geosmin or a geosmin source in a non-beet matrix). If tradition bearers identify such a sample as borscht, H2 (interchangeability of function carriers) receives the strongest possible support within this series; rejection localizes the boundary of substitutability. The set is completed by adversarial counterexamples: a constructed dish with formally high ИБ that tradition bearers are expected not to call borscht, and a canonically named borscht with deliberately reduced ИБ. Disagreement between index and panel on such samples diagnoses holes in the model more strongly than agreement on prototypes. Categorical responses are additionally collected from an expanded panel of tradition bearers (at least 30 people), and generalizability of the weights is tested by a leave-one-recipe-out scheme—training without one regional borscht type and then classifying the unseen recipe.

**Metrics:** a blind sensory panel answering the identification question (“Would you classify this sample as borscht?”), supplemented with a graded similarity question (“How similar is this sample to borscht?”, 0–10) for comparability with the paired-study protocol [45], and a rating of **confidence** in the classification (0–3). The measures are separated into two quantities: **membership** (“how much is this borscht?”), with two indicators of the latent variable—binary identification and graded similarity—and **boundary certainty** (“how confident am I in the classification?”). Confidence is intentionally separated from the latent membership variable because an answer “definitely not borscht” with confidence 3 shows that confidence measures certainty of the boundary rather than direction of membership. An additional uncertainty metric is the entropy of the binary decision from the proportion p of positive responses: H = −p·log₂p − (1−p)·log₂(1−p), maximal at a true class boundary (p ≈ 0.5). Peripheral samples should show elevated entropy together with reduced boundary certainty. Colorimetry is also collected. The planned analysis compares the identification proportion across variants: the model predicts a high proportion for variants (a)–(d) when K/S/T are preserved, a sharp decline in the control with disrupted K, and a moderate decline with disrupted Ц¹ (H1–H3); shchi (variant f) is expected to be rejected by the S₁ < 0.5 filter (Section 8.1).

**Calibration without circularity.** The ИБ weights and threshold are calibrated on these data (Section 8), so the sample is divided in advance into **training and validation subsets by cooking batch and day** (not by individual ratings, because ratings from one batch are dependent): logistic-regression weights and the ROC threshold are determined only on the training subset, while classification performance is assessed on held-out batches and days. Panel responses serve as the **“gold standard”** — a cultural category is, by definition, measured through recognition by its bearers; documented historical-cultural affiliation of the dish (Section 8.1) serves as an independent external criterion, which is why a convergent analogue (gazpacho) is included.

**[Г] Hypothesis:** identification of a dish as borscht will correlate not with the presence of beet but with preservation of the sour-sweet balance and technology, directly testing the model in Section 8. The historical experience of *luosong tang* serves here not as proof but as **motivation for the hypothesis**: it is a strong documented example of cultural transformation of the dish consistent with functional substitutability of beet [17]. Whether *luosong tang* itself should be recognized as borscht is not a premise of the study but one of the empirical questions the experiment is intended to answer.

### Venue and schedule: restaurant format (borscht week)

The experimental series is designed to be conducted in a working restaurant and, together with the paired shchi program [45], forms a single two-week cycle: week one—shchi [45]; **week two—borscht** (the present program). The rationale is common to both studies: ecological validity (soups are prepared and evaluated under professional-kitchen conditions), reduced dependence among replicates (batches are spread across different days—this reduces but does not eliminate their dependence: common ingredient lots and kitchen regime are accounted for by random effects of batch and day in the statistical model), and routine control of temperature, mass, and time. The requirement of n = 5 independent batches per variant is met across repeated days within the week; serving temperature is controlled according to variant type (hot and cold borscht, each at its own standardized temperature).

The borscht-week schedule is: before the cycle—Experiment 0 (survey of at least 30 practitioners of home and professional cooking); days 1–2—Experiment 1 (beet types); days 3–4—Experiment 2 (acid sources; sensory analysis using vector A = (I, C, D, P, R) [45]); day 5—Experiment 3 (borscht without beet; shchi from the first week of the cycle as the cross-dish negative control). CIELAB colorimetry and pH measurement are performed on site; samples for repeat colorimetry at 24 and 48 h are stored at 4 °C (Experiment 2). Sensory-panel composition and sample coding follow the general design requirements; as during shchi week, tasting by restaurant patrons under informed consent may be used to expand the sample. All beet types, Swiss chard, and beet greens are procured in advance as part of the preparation phase shared with shchi week (which includes fermenting cabbage and green tomatoes [45]).

### How to interpret negative results

A null or negative result does not necessarily falsify the model as a whole; it localizes failure to a specific link. The following interpretive table is specified in advance:

| Removing beet sharply lowers identification even when K/S/T are preserved (H2) | functional substitutability of the carrier for this panel | historically documented substitutions (*luosong tang* [17]); the disagreement localizes the issue to categorization, not chemistry |

## 8. Theoretical Model: the Borscht-ness Index

**[Г]** The analysis above can be generalized as follows: borscht is defined not by one ingredient but by a combination of structural features. A formalization is proposed—the **borscht-ness index (ИБ)**, calculated across five axes, each scored from 0 to 1:

**ИБ = w₁·O + w₂·K + w₃₁·S₁ + w₃₂·S₂ + w₄·T + w₅·Ц¹**

where:

- **O (base)** — presence of a rich liquid base with vegetable filling;
- **K (acidity)** — presence of a pronounced acidic component, regardless of origin;
- **S₁ (sweetness)** — the sweet component of the root-vegetable flavor profile (beet, carrot, parsnip, and their substitutes);
- **S₂ (earthiness)** — the earthy background associated with geosmin. Components S₁ and S₂ are deliberately separated and enter the index as **independent terms** with separate weights: they are chemically and sensorially distinct—sugar beet maximizes S₁ while having a different S₂ level from table beet—and in the quantitative version of the model they are assessed separately (Section 7). Earlier versions collapsed them into a composite axis S = (S₁, S₂); the present notation makes that composite structure explicit;
- **T (technology)** — characteristic technology as an internal property of the process: light sautéing/slow stewing of vegetables, dressed-soup structure, thermal integration of components. Serving ritual is not part of axis T; it is an external cultural modifier (layer M, Section 4.4);
- **Ц¹ (observed color)** — the red-burgundy color of the dish as a measurable quantity (CIELAB, above all a*; the most variable axis, absent in green and white borscht). Ц¹ is fundamentally different from **Ц²—the pigment source** (beet / tomatoes / absent), recorded by coordinate Ц of the type code (Section 4.2): two dishes with equal Ц¹ may have different Ц². Separating these variables makes hypothesis H7 possible (Section 7). The index contains Ц¹ specifically—the model describes perceived structure rather than origin;
- **w₁…w₅, w₃₁, w₃₂** — weighting coefficients, with w₅ (pigment) the smallest, reflecting the optional status of red color, while the sweetness pair is split as w₃₁ + w₃₂ (for an a priori w₃ = 0.25, for example 0.15 + 0.10: sweetness as the necessary flavor pole receives greater weight than background earthiness).

**Notation note (alignment with the paired study).** The pigment axis is denoted Ц¹, in accordance with the color axis of the type code (Section 4.2), whose coordinate records the pigment source (Ц²); the superscript separates measured color from its origin. An earlier version used the letter P, but in the acid-profile vector A = (I, C, D, P, R) of the paired study [45], P denotes temporal position of acidity. The collision has therefore been removed for joint reading of the two studies. The remaining letters are aligned semantically: O (base), K (acidity), and T (technology) are shared between ИБ and ИЩ [45]; sweetness axis S and pigment axis Ц are specific to borscht, while axis N (broth richness) is specific to the shchi model, where it is separated from O as an independent coordinate.

**Operationalization of the scales.** Each axis is scored on a three-point ordinal scale with anchors:

- O: 0 — water; 0.5 — weak stock; 1 — rich broth;
- K: 0 — acidic component absent; 0.5 — background acidity; 1 — pronounced acidity;
- S₁: 0 — sweetness absent; 0.5 — background sweetness; 1 — pronounced root-vegetable sweetness;
- S₂: 0 — earthy background absent; 0.5 — background earthiness; 1 — pronounced earthiness (geosmin note);
- T: 0 — different technology (including assembled okroshka-type structure); 0.5 — partial match; 1 — full set (slow stewing/light sautéing of vegetables, dressed-soup structure, thermal integration);
- Ц¹: 0 — red color absent; 0.5 — pink or orange tone; 1 — saturated ruby color (in colorimetry, normalized a*).

**Status of the model.** In the present study, ИБ is a **preliminary (heuristic) model of the structural borscht-ness index**, not a calibrated measurement instrument and not a ready-made criterion of truth. The weights w = (0.2; 0.3; 0.15 + 0.10; 0.15; 0.1), with dominance of the acidity and sweetness axes (the latter decomposed as w₃₁·S₁ + w₃₂·S₂), are not empirically established coefficients: they form an **a priori model intended for subsequent calibration** on sensory-panel data (Section 7). The threshold ИБ ≥ 0.6 has the same status: it is an a priori threshold of the pilot model. The empirical threshold must be determined from the data—for example, as the value optimally separating panel responses “borscht” / “not borscht” on the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve; only after such calibration would a specific number acquire substantive meaning. The calibration procedure is defined explicitly. Binary responses of the blind panel in Experiment 3 (“Would you classify this sample as borscht?”) serve as the **operational criterion**—the term “gold standard” is deliberately not used: 8–12 tasters constitute a working reference for laboratory calibration, not a gold standard for the cultural category as such (population-level validation is the task of the expanded panel of tradition bearers, Experiment 0). Weights are estimated from coefficients of logistic regression of identification on axes O, K, S₁, S₂, T, Ц¹—symmetrically with calibration of ИЩ in Experiment 3 of the paired study [45], where weights are inferred from panel data rather than assigned by experts—and the ИБ threshold is selected on the ROC curve as the optimal separation point for panel responses. Both weights and threshold are determined only on the training subset (split by cooking batches and days, Section 7) and then tested on held-out data; otherwise calibration would be circular. Class assignment is a two-stage procedure: the necessary filter K ≥ 0.5 and S₁ ≥ 0.5, followed by integral score ИБ ≥ 0.6 for the class core (see Section 8.1). The single working name used below is “borscht-ness index” (ИБ).

**Collinearity of axes.** The weighted sum assumes separable contributions from the axes, yet the axes are correlated through multifunctional carriers (beet simultaneously shifts Ц¹, S₁, S₂, and T; tomatoes shift Ц¹ and K; see Section 4.2). Regression weights estimated on correlated predictors are unstable. Therefore calibration (Section 7) includes multicollinearity diagnostics (VIF) and, if substantial, regularization (ridge) or transition to latent variables (factor analysis); in the latter case, final weights belong to latent factors rather than to the original axes. The same assumption and solution are adopted in the paired study [45].

**Mapping the type code onto the index.** The type code Б = (Ц, К, Б, Т, С) from Section 4.3 and the ИБ index are tools with different functions; their relationship is made explicit to avoid double-counting the same properties:

| Code coordinate | Index axis | Nature of mapping |
|---|---|---|
| Ц (color source, Ц1–Ц4/Ц0) | Ц¹ (observed color) | not identity: the code records *origin* of pigment (Ц²), the index records *measured* color; relation tested by H7 |
| К (acidity source, К1–К8) | K (acidity) | the source determines acid profile C, but axis K measures *magnitude* of the acidic component (sensory + pH/TA), not its origin |
| Б (base, Б1–Б5) | O (base) | direct mapping: base type determines position on the “water → rich broth” scale |
| Т (beet treatment, Т0–Т5) | T (technology) | partial: axis T is broader, including the dressed-soup structure and thermal integration of all vegetables, not only beet treatment |
| С (garnish composition, С1–С5) | — | excluded from the index: garnish composition distinguishes varieties within the class rather than degree of class membership |

The table makes the division of functions explicit: the type code is a taxonomic instrument (what kind of borscht this is), whereas the index is a measurement instrument (how fully the dish realizes the class features); origins of features (Ц², acidity source) remain in the code, while their perceived consequences (Ц¹, K) enter the index.

A dish is assigned to the borscht class when the aggregate ИБ exceeds the threshold, without requiring a maximum on any single axis. The model’s central thesis is:

> **[Г] Beet is not a necessary element of borscht, but one means of simultaneously achieving values on axes Ц, S, and T.** Historically, this means proved the most efficient: one root vegetable simultaneously provides color, sweetness, and the characteristic earthy background, reducing the need for separate components to build the overall flavor profile. This explains its dominance, but not exclusivity.

**[Г]** The model explains the observed diversity: green borscht (K and T with zero Ц¹), white borscht (K, T with low S₁, S₂, and Ц¹), tomato borscht and *luosong tang* (K, S₁, Ц¹ without beet) [17], lean Ashkenazi borscht (K, S₁, Ц¹ with variable O) [14], clear *bortchok* (minimal T-filling with maximal Ц¹) [9], [13], classic borscht (maximum on all axes), and the historical sour soups from hogweed and berries [4].

### 8.1. Class boundaries: core, periphery, and falsifiability

**Two-stage assignment procedure.** Assignment of a dish to the borscht class occurs in two steps, reconciling the integral sum with threshold conditions and avoiding conflict between them:

1. **Necessary filter:** K ≥ 0.5 and S₁ ≥ 0.5—a pronounced sour-sweet axis. The filter applies to the sweetness component S₁ rather than to a composite S axis: earthiness S₂ is a background modifier, not a necessary condition (white borscht retains S₁ at low S₂). A dish failing the filter cannot be assigned to the class core regardless of its total ИБ.
2. **Integral assessment:** among dishes that pass the filter, ИБ ≥ 0.6 defines the **core** of the class; dishes with ИБ 0.4–0.6, as well as dishes that formally fail the filter but belong to a documented lineage of the family, form the **periphery** (see below).

Thus, K and S₁ thresholds are necessary conditions, while ИБ ≥ 0.6 is a criterion of completeness of membership: the mechanisms do not compete but are applied sequentially. The pigment axis Ц is not necessary (green and white borscht varieties).

**General principle for separating adjacent classes.** The class boundary is not defined by enumerating “non-borschts,” but by a general principle: borscht is a **thermally integrated dressed soup with a sour-sweet axis**. Specific exclusions follow from this:

- shchi lacks the sweet-earthy root-vegetable axis (S₁ < 0.5). The mirror distinction is also formalized by the paired model: in the Shchi Index [45], acidity is an optional K axis with the expected lowest weight and no binary assignment threshold, whereas here the sour-sweet axis is a necessary filter. The asymmetry is substantive: shchi identity is carried above all by slow-stewing technology and vegetable mass, while borscht identity is carried by flavor balance and the pigment-related function of acidity; this is why borscht, rather than shchi, has threshold conditions;
- rassolnik has acidity (К2) but lacks the sweet-earthy profile (S₁ < 0.5);
- solyanka has a different flavor axis (salty-smoky) and a different technological structure;
- botvinya and beet okroshka formally pass K and S₁ but are excluded by axis T: they are **assembled okroshka-type cold soups**, whose components are combined after preparation without integrative stewing or a dressed-soup structure (T < 0.5). The essential distinction is that cold beet soup is borscht that has cooled (its components underwent integrative thermal treatment, Т4), whereas botvinya is okroshka enriched with beet greens.

**Core and periphery.** The model directly predicts peripheral members—dishes included in the family not by structure but by **documented genetic lineage and self-designation**. Canonical examples are white borscht (*barszcz biały* on rye sourdough—pronounced acidity with an almost zero sweet-earthy axis) and clear *barszcz czysty* (minimal garnish, С5). Both fail the strict core filter, and the model records rather than conceals this: they belong to the family as its periphery. A testable consequence follows: **peripheral cases should be precisely those that generate disputes among tradition bearers** (“Is this really borscht?”), whereas consensus should be maximal for the core. This agrees with observed reality and is subject to quantitative testing in Experiment 3.

**Two levels of the model: structural borscht-ness and historical-cultural affiliation.** The preceding argument requires an explicit terminological distinction between two concepts measured by the model in different ways:

1. **Structural borscht-ness**—the degree to which a dish corresponds to the model parameters (axes, filter, ИБ). It is a continuous measurable quantity: structurally, a dish may be “more” or “less” borscht-like.
2. **Historical-cultural affiliation**—inclusion of the dish in the borscht family by usage, self-designation, and documented genealogy. This is a binary social fact that cannot be derived from structure alone.

The second level is not speculative: it is operationalized using three independent indicators—(a) the dish’s self-designation among tradition bearers; (b) documented genealogy (cookbooks, menus, dictionary records [5], [7], [9]); and (c) name recognition measured by survey (Experiment 0) and panel (Experiment 3). The distinction between the two levels must itself be understood precisely: it is generated by the threshold logic of “core/periphery” in the present model—in the threshold-free shchi model [45], the need for this distinction does not arise—so it is a property of the model, not an established property of the object. The reverse direction of causality is also possible: threshold logic may historically have developed in borscht precisely in response to the high cultural load carried by affiliation (diaspora branches, Section 1.3). The causal direction remains open.

The full class model can therefore be stated as follows: **borscht is not a class defined by a necessary set of ingredients, but a historically evolved family of dishes; structural parameters measure degree of similarity to its prototypical core but do not replace the historical-cultural criterion of affiliation.** Under this distinction, peripheral cases (white borscht, *barszcz czysty*) cease to be “exceptions that had to be rescued”: they are theoretically predicted—family members with high historical-cultural affiliation but reduced structural borscht-ness. This prediction is also consistent with lexical history: *barszcz* long served as an umbrella designation for sour soups in general [4], so the family periphery naturally extends beyond the structural core.

**Structure and genealogy: refining the scope of the model.** The structural criterion alone does not distinguish *luosong tang* from convergently similar dishes—for example, gazpacho or an Italian tomato soup with carrot and onion, which formally yield similar values on Ц3, К5, S. The model must acknowledge its actual scope: it describes not a universal class of “all soups with this structure,” but a **historically linked family**, in the sense of Ludwig Wittgenstein’s “family resemblance” [39]: a class with no single necessary feature, whose members are linked by a network of overlapping similarities and a shared lineage. Structure determines degree of membership within the family; genealogy separates the family from convergent analogues. Gazpacho is not borscht not because it has “the wrong structure,” but because it has a different lineage. Whether *luosong tang* is borscht is not decided a priori: its genealogy is documented [17], while the degree to which tradition bearers recognize it as borscht is an empirical question (Experiment 3). The genealogical criterion is itself testable rather than speculative: it is tested directly by including a convergent analogue (gazpacho) among the samples in Experiment 3 (Section 7).

**Prototype and family resemblance: a hybrid framework.** The model deliberately combines two distinct theories of categories, and they should not be treated as synonyms. Prototype theory (E. Rosch) assumes a central exemplar and graded membership around it; it describes **structural borscht-ness**—degree of similarity to the core along the axes and threshold. Family resemblance (L. Wittgenstein) assumes a network of overlapping features without a single center; it describes the **scope of the family**, in which peripheral members are included by genealogy rather than structure. The prototype framework is primary because it is the measurement framework; the Wittgensteinian framework answers a different question: why the class has a periphery that cannot be reduced to the core. In the paired shchi study [45], the threshold-free ИЩ model is likewise primarily prototypical, while the Wittgensteinian formulation serves as a framework for harmonizing the two studies.

**Arbiter of falsifiability.** “Tradition bearers” are defined operationally as practitioners for whom dishes of the family belong to a regular (everyday or festive) repertoire. Because there are several traditions (Russian, Polish, Ashkenazi, etc.), the family boundary is empirically variable—and the model predicts precisely this: disagreement is expected at the periphery, not in the core. The falsification condition is therefore refined accordingly: the model is falsified if (a) tradition bearers systematically classify as borscht dishes outside the sour-sweet axis and dressed-soup technology, or (b) consensus on the core is lower than consensus on the periphery.

To permit causal interpretation of the experimental contrasts in Section 7, an explicit causal scheme is specified (symmetric to the scheme in the paired study [45]):

Each arrow’s confounding variables are paired with controls in the matrix in Section 7; an unaccounted confounder makes the corresponding arrow non-identifiable, as explicitly noted in the negative-result interpretation table (Section 7).

### 8.3. The model within the trilogy: three configurations of category

**[Г]** The borscht-ness index forms a methodological trilogy with the ИЩ [45] and ИО [46] models; the models are not reducible to one another:

| Parameter | ИЩ (shchi) [45] | ИБ (borscht) | ИО (okroshka) [46] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Constitutive feature | technology (slow stewing), vegetable mass | flavor balance (K, S₁) | physical parameters (Х, Н) |
| Necessary filter | absent | flavor: K ≥ 0.5 and S₁ ≥ 0.5 | physical: Х ≥ 0.5 and Н ≥ 0.5 |
| Core threshold | absent (threshold-free model) | ИБ ≥ 0.6 | ИО ≥ 0.6 |
| Acidity | optional axis, expected weakest | necessary filter, hypothesized strongest predictor | variable axis with replaceable carrier (axis K + code О) |
| Multifunctional carrier | sauerkraut (V, K, T) | beet (Ц¹, S₁, S₂, T) | kvass (O, K, Г) |
| Type code | Щ = (O, V, K, T, N) | Б = (Ц, К, Б, Т, С) | Ок = (О, Х, Н, П, К) |
| Convergent analogue control | kapuśniak | gazpacho / *luosong tang* | tarator / *šaltibarščiai* / gazpacho |

*Note to the table.* In the “multifunctional carrier” row for ИО, carbonation (Г) is listed: it is a modifier, not an index axis—kvass covers axes O and K and modifier Г, but does not cover axes Х, Н, П, З; the ИЩ and ИБ rows list axes only. Identically named letters in different models do not necessarily denote the same measurement variable: for example, axis O in ИЩ spans “water → rich broth,” whereas in ИО it spans “neutral water → fermented acidic base.” In joint reading they should be distinguished as O_щ, O_б, O_ок. A unified metalanguage of features for the trilogy is the subject of a separate methodological study in the project.

The hypotheses of the trilogy are mirrored and mutually testable: acidity is a weak axis in shchi, a strong predictor in borscht, and has a replaceable carrier in okroshka; compensatory architecture is shared (P6/P8 in [45], H8 in the present study, H8 in [46]); and cross-class tests (removing acid from all three dishes; serving okroshka hot) are feasible within one restaurant cycle (Section 7). Full comparison of the three architectures is the subject of a joint methodological article in the project; the present table is a working scaffold. The striking symmetry of the three configurations (no filter / flavor filter / physical filter) is explicitly declared a **hypothesis of the trilogy, not an established fact**: the models may have been partly selected to fit this symmetry, and the experimental program is allowed to destroy it.

---

## 9. Discussion

The proposed approach shifts the research perspective from recipe to structure. Borscht appears not as a fixed set of ingredients but as a **class of culinary systems united by a particular balance of flavor, color, and cooking technology**. Historical evidence is consistent with this being the original nature of the phenomenon: even in the nineteenth century *barszcz* was a generic designation for sour soups [4]; dictionary fixation of a beet norm was relatively late [5]; and the printed culinary tradition from the second half of the eighteenth century already treated borscht as a dish with many stable variants [7], [9]. These data permit—but do not themselves causally prove—the hypothesis that the “class-like” nature of borscht is primary, while its specification around beet resulted from historical selection of the most efficient function carrier; testing the causal part of this claim belongs to the experimental program (Section 7). The reverse is also true: historical evidence constrains the model. Any version of the model predicting an ingredient-based (beet-based) definition of the class would already be contradicted by the documentary history [4], [5], [7], [9], even without experiment.

Diaspora trajectories function as an independent check on the model: when the dish migrated into new cultural and climatic conditions, the carrier changed (beet → tomatoes in Shanghai [17]; meat base → lean base under kashrut [14]) while structure and identity were retained. The fact that both Shanghai diners and bearers of Ashkenazi tradition continue to recognize the dish as “borscht” / “Russian soup” suggests that identification proceeds through structural features rather than composition.

Chemical evidence confirms the internal coherence of the system: the acidic component of borscht simultaneously (a) stabilizes red betacyanins, which are stable specifically under acidic conditions [19], [23], [24]; (b) chemically suppresses the earthy geosmin note [30]; and (c) forms the flavor counterweight to root-vegetable sweetness [1]. Acidity is thus a hidden structural backbone of the dish, whereas beet is the most economical—but replaceable—carrier of the remaining functions.

The question posed in this study—“what minimal features make an object itself?”—moves the investigation beyond cooking. It is a question about hidden structure behind an external phenomenon. Borscht is a convenient experimental object for this kind of inquiry: unlike abstract systems, it permits direct laboratory testing through tables, colorimetry, tasting, and graphs.

**Limitations of the study:** this is a theoretical-analytical investigation; the experimental series (Section 7) remains a program and must confirm or falsify the working parameters of the model. The weights and threshold of the borscht-ness index are proposed as calibratable hypotheses, not measurement results. The basic similarity metric (Section 4.5) is unweighted and therefore coarse; its weighted version depends on the same calibration. The source base is uneven: theoretical claims rely on peer-reviewed food-chemistry studies, whereas historical and practical blocks partly use institutional publications and primary documents of tradition (see the source-critical note in Section 2). The classification of regional variants does not claim completeness. Joint tasks of the paired studies—a shared theoretical frame “chemistry → sensory perception → category,” a unified apparatus of axes and thresholds, a joint historical chronology, and mapping of regional variability of both dishes—are deliberately placed outside the present study and reserved for a separate methodological article in the project.

## 10. Conclusions

1. **[Г]** Borscht is not a recipe but a class of culinary systems united by the balance of acidity, sweetness, a rich base, and characteristic technology; red pigment is an optional axis.
2. **[Д → П]** Beet simultaneously forms color, sweetness, and the characteristic earthy background and is therefore an optimal but not necessary component; the etymology and history of the dish (hogweed → beet), together with diaspora practices (beet → tomatoes in *luosong tang*), are consistent with the fundamental replaceability of function carriers.
3. **[Г]** The acidic component is proposed as the hypothetical structural backbone of borscht (H1): chemically, it forms flavor, stabilizes betacyanin coloration (stable at pH 3–7), and neutralizes geosmin earthiness, while its status as the strongest predictor of identification as borscht is subject to direct experimental testing.
4. **[Д]** The cultural significance of borscht spans every level—from the first printed description (1779) and imperial canonization of “Russian cuisine” in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries through Nicholas II’s coronation menu to restaurants of Russian Shanghai and space rations; at every level the dish retains identity while composition changes, supporting the structural rather than ingredient-based nature of its identity.
5. **[Д]** Beet types (table, fodder, sugar, yellow, greens) constitute a full-fledged research variable: pigment and sugar content differ among them by multiples, predictably affecting dish color and flavor.
6. **[Г]** The proposed preliminary borscht-ness index formalizes the degree of structural similarity between a dish and the prototypical core of the borscht family while explicitly distinguishing two levels—structural borscht-ness and historical-cultural affiliation. The weights and threshold are a priori and are to be calibrated using CIELAB colorimetry, pH measurement, and sensory analysis.
7. **[Г]** The scientific novelty of the study lies in its organization of knowledge: it proposes the first parametric classification of borscht along five functional axes, a functional “component → functions” matrix, the formal type code Б = (Ц, К, Б, Т, С), and a variety-similarity metric (Hamming distance over code coordinates as an explicit baseline, with typed, weighted, and Euclidean generalizations), moving comparison among borscht variants from opinion to measurement.
8. **[Г]** The ИБ model forms a methodological pair with the Shchi Index (ИЩ) in [45], but the models are not reducible to one another: ИБ includes pigment (Ц¹) and sweetness (S₁, S₂) axes and a two-stage assignment procedure (filter K ≥ 0.5 and S₁ ≥ 0.5, followed by core threshold ИБ ≥ 0.6), whereas ИЩ deliberately has no binary threshold, includes axis N (broth richness), which is absent from ИБ, and is used in conjunction with vector A = (I, C, D, P, R), which decomposes the acid profile into temporal components (D, P). ИЩ itself does not contain those components, but its experimental program collects data on vector A, for which ИБ has no analogue. The hypotheses of the pair are mirrored and mutually testable within the same experimental form: acidity is the hypothesized strongest predictor of borscht identification (H1) and the expected weakest ИЩ axis; cross-class H6 converts this mirrored comparison into a direct joint test by removing the acidic component from both dishes.
